### George sells land interests to Lizzie Douglas, April 1, 1887

[Scan page 2 is an identifying label: certificate of record, land sale and deed to Lizzie Douglas by George Douglas, April 1, 1887.]

Texas, Ellis County.

I, George Douglas of Ellis County, acknowledge receiving seven hundred dollars from Lizzie Douglas. For that payment I sell and convey all my right, title, claim, and interest in an eighty-acre tract within the 1,280-acre A. J. Porter survey. It was surveyed for Rebecca Winniford's heirs. My interest came through deeds from Mary F. Roper, Alice E. McCrary, E. L. Winniford, and H. O. Winniford, and consists of an undivided sixty-acre interest in the eighty-acre Winniford tract.

The land is in Ellis County, about ten miles N 30° E of Waxahachie, on Sublett's Creek. Its boundaries are retained below in the survey's original units, varas.

- Begin at a stake in the middle of Cottonwood Branch, the Winniford tract's southeast corner. An elm bears S 67° W, 20 varas; a cedar bears S 22° E, 22 varas.
- S 60° W, 1,337 varas to a stake on the Porter survey's west line.
- N 30° W, along that line, 246 varas to the southwest corner of a church lot.
- N 60° E, 280 varas to the church lot's southeast corner.
- N 30° W, 90 varas to its northeast corner.
- N 60° E, 1,094 varas to a stake on the Porter survey's north line; an elm bears N 61° E, 95 varas.
- S 30° E, 230 varas to Cottonwood Branch.
- Down the branch along its bends, 120 varas to the starting point: eighty acres, more or less.

[Scan page 4 also begins a second conveyance.]

For a further three hundred dollars paid by Lizzie, I convey all my right, title, claim, and interest in twenty acres of the same A. J. Porter survey. This came to me through a deed from Mary Douglas jointly to Jesse, Warren, and myself; subsequent deeds from Jesse and Warren; and a deed from Lizzie dated the same day as this instrument.

[Scan page 5]

The twenty acres are described as follows:

- Begin at the Winniford tract's southeast corner, a stake in the middle of Cottonwood Branch; an elm bears S 67° W, 20 varas, and a cedar S 22° W, 22 varas. [The cedar bearing differs from the earlier description and is retained as written.]
- S 60° W, 976 varas to a stake at the original northwest corner of Lizzie Douglas's eighty-acre tract; elms bear S 75° W, 9 varas, and S 80° W, 9 varas.
- S 30° E, 113 varas to a stake on Lizzie's west line.
- N 60° E, 1,096 varas to the middle of Cottonwood Branch; elms bear S 75° W, 23⅘ varas, and S 29½° W, 15⅓ varas [the last fraction is difficult].
- Up the branch along its bends to the starting point: twenty acres, more or less.

Lizzie, her heirs, and assigns are to hold this land and all associated rights permanently. I bind myself, my heirs, and assigns to warrant and defend the title against lawful claims.

Signed George Douglas, April 1, 1887.

## Pages 12-15
### L. A. Jetton to Lizzie: family news and Rebecca's estate, March 20, 1883

[Page 12 contains the envelope to Miss Lizzie Douglas, Dallas, Texas, postmarked Readyville, March 23, and an identifying note.]

Readyville, March 20, 1883. My dearest cousin Lizzie,

Your kind letter arrived, and I was glad to hear you were all well and had not forgotten me. Willie and I are as well as usual. Our relatives and friends are fairly well. Mary Lee's baby, little Granville, has been very sick but is better today. Papa's health has improved a great deal; I think he looks better than he has for months.

Adele spent several days with me last week. She looks well and is very pleased with her new home. Brother McGuffy has moved into his new home. They have a very nice house and yard and are pleased with their surroundings. I am glad Jennie lives so near me—only half a mile away. We visit often. Aunt Sallie is still in West Tennessee. We have not heard from her for some time, so I do not know whether she will come back this spring.

We are doing some gardening, but the cold weather has kept our vegetables from doing much good. We had quite a snowstorm yesterday. The snow is all gone this morning, and it is pleasant outdoors in the sun.

I am lonely here, with only a young Black child for company. Willie is home at night but away every day, working land at his grandfather's this year or hauling with his wagon. So I am very confined to the house and lonely.

You asked about Cousin Rebecca Alexander's death. I did not know she was sick until the night before she died. At the funeral I learned that she had gone out very early in the mornings to look at the comet, which interested her greatly. She caught a severe cold and died from its effects. I think she remained in her right mind throughout her illness. That morning she sat up, ate breakfast, and talked with her friends; she died that evening a few days later [the writer's phrasing is awkward].

I know little about the lawsuit, only what I hear others say. The latest, most reliable information came from Charlie. He and Papa visited Cousin Andy last Saturday and found him in poor health and much distress. Andy told them Cousin Rebecca's will had been stolen from her drawer two weeks before her death. He is willing to surrender the land and does not want a lawsuit, but wants to buy it from the heirs. He has already arranged to obtain several Wright family members' interests. The Isaac Wright estate is said to be worth about $2,000. I think the heirs ought to have it, since Cousin Andy will be a rich man without it.

My best love to you all. Write soon. Your affectionate cousin, L. A. Jetton.

Lizzie, please do not let anyone see this poorly written letter. I am ashamed of it. I have so little practice in writing that I cannot do it as I once did.

[Editorial note: The account of Rebecca's illness and the missing will is the letter writer's report, including hearsay. The source's historical racial term has been rendered as “Black.”]

## Pages 16-18
### L. A. Jetton to Lizzie: illness, a wedding, and sympathy for Sue's death

[Page 16 labels this March 3, 1878. The handwritten year on page 17 is difficult and can also look like 1898; the label is not treated as decisive.]

Readyville, Tennessee, March 3. My very dear cousin Lizzie,

Your kind, interesting letter arrived, and I hasten to reply. The family is in rather poor health. Papa and Mother are both unwell, and all the children have whooping cough. Willie has it very badly and has also had an attack of bilious fever. This morning he is sitting up a little for the first time in several days. Adele is quite sick with the cough for the second time. Cousin Willie is improving, but Charlie's children are very ill.

The wedding took place as planned. We had nice cake and a good supper, and everything went quietly. Adele, Willie, and I attended the reception at Cousin Miriam's. They served a splendid dinner, and everyone seemed to enjoy it. Jimmie and Mary will live with Cousin Miriam, who is almost alone: her only unmarried child is hardly ever home.

Jimmie and Mary came yesterday evening. Both are well and have gone to church at New Hope today. We teased them this morning about their family relationships. They both call their mothers-in-law “Cousin.” They say the relationships are so mixed up that they do not know how to sort them out, so they will use the same names as before the marriage.

Lizzie, Jimmie was sixteen when we moved here. When I first met him, I jokingly told Mary Belle that I had chosen him for my son-in-law. She was indignant and said she would not have him. Now I laugh at her for changing her mind. She says people often do that nowadays.

Our relatives and friends are generally well, as far as I know. We have had so much rainy weather this winter that we hardly get more than one or two good days at a time. Farmers are discouraged because they cannot plow. The wheat looks very good.

Dear cousin, you have my deepest sympathy in your great sorrow. I loved Cousin Sue on earth and hope to meet her in heaven, where sorrow and parting are unknown.

I must close. Tell Uncle Jimmie that Mary will write soon and send pictures of herself and Jimmie. Give my best love to the family and take a large share yourself. Write as often as you can; I am always glad to receive a letter. Adele says to tell Cousin George she will write soon, once she is well of the whooping cough. The children all send much love.

Your affectionate cousin, L. A. Jetton.

Please excuse this letter. The children have been making such a noise, with whooping and coughing, that I could hardly write. I will try to do better next time. Yours as ever, L. A. J.

## Pages 19-22
### Mary Lee Jetton to Lizzie, April 8, 1874

[Page 19 is an identifying label.]

Readyville, April 8, 1874. Dear cousin Lizzie,

It has rained all morning, so I did not go to school and thought this a good opportunity to write. We are all well, and I believe our relatives are fairly healthy except Aunt Kittie. She has been very sick for some time. The doctor thinks she is improving a little, but she fears she has consumption. Aunt Sarah visited a few days ago and seemed in splendid health. Uncle Charlie has grown so large you would hardly recognize him: he weighs two hundred pounds.

Uncle Tip went to Nashville last Monday to buy his new spring goods. Grandpa went with him and returned last night. Mr. Barr's youngest son is to marry Miss Nannie Felker [surname uncertain], Dr. Dickens's niece, tomorrow night. I have an invitation and think I will attend. Nannie is a very nice girl, and I suppose Aunt Jane and Mr. Barr will be pleased with the match.

There have been many parties around here this winter, mostly play parties and only a few dances. Several church members have been dancing. We have preaching at New Hope Church on the first Sunday of each month, and Sunday school every Sunday evening. Mama and Uncle Albert are the teachers.

Little Jennie Whitt [surname uncertain], Gran's [name or family title uncertain], is staying here and going to school. She and Delie have a fine time together. Delie is almost as big as I am. Willie is growing some, but not very fast. He attends school and seems to love his books. Joanna has had her flower garden made and enjoys helping prepare the beds and plant flowers.

I have started a scrapbook. Tell Cousin George to send some pretty verses for it. Dear cousin, you do not know how much I would like to see my old home and friends again. When I first came away, I thought I would soon forget you, but I have not. I think of you often and want to see you more than ever.

Give my love to all the family and keep some for yourself. Write soon. Yours truly, Mary Lee Jetton.

## Pages 23-25
### L. A. Jetton to Lizzie: school, church, and family visits, February 1, 1874

[Page 23 is an identifying label.]

Readyville, Tennessee, February 1, 1874. Dear cousin Lizzie,

Your kind, welcome letter arrived, and I hasten to reply. We are all well, as are our relatives and friends as far as I know. The neighborhood has been healthy this winter. It has been very mild, with much rain and little snow.

The young people have had a lively winter, with more parties and big dinners than I can keep track of. The children went to two last week, at Charlie's and Bell's. I hope they are finished for now, because school starts next week and they must stop running about. One of our preaching brothers will teach at Readyville. He has taught for years and is a fine scholar and a good man.

We have preaching at New Hope once a month. Brother Rice Sewell is preaching for us again this year. Three other churches have preaching on different days, so we can go every Sunday if we wish.

Mr. Barr and Aunt Jane stayed overnight with us last week. They were fairly well, and their children were well. Aunt Jane is much worn down and looks old and feeble. Mr. Barr looks much the same as he has for the last five years.

Mary Lee went to Readyville last week and had her picture taken to send to Grandpa. She planned to write to you this evening, but several of her beaus have arrived, so she must postpone it.

I was very surprised by the money you sent. I had never imagined your mother would sell the saddle. But, as you say, money never comes amiss, and I was very glad to get it.

Charlie and his family are doing well. Little Alma is sweet and speaks plainly. She is very fond of her grandmother. Charlie is still my brother and friend, and I would visit him often, poor fellow, but I have less use for his wife than any woman I ever knew. I try to be friendly with her for his sake, but she has done so many mean, low things [the original calls them “low-down dirty tricks”].

Write soon and tell us all the news. Give my love to the family and tell them that, if I live, I mean to come see them before long. The children send much love. I must close as it is late. Ever your friend and cousin, L. A. Jetton.

Mr. Eddie Tally, one of Emma's brothers, has been visiting Mary Lee. I do not know what Peter will say about it.

## Pages 26-29
### Lummie Goodloe to her cousin, June 21, 1874

[Page 26 identifies the writer as Lummie (Columbia Goodloe) and the recipient as Lizzie Douglas. These are the label's identifications; the letter signs “Lummie.”]

Russellville, Alabama, June 21, 1874. My dear cousin,

Your letter has arrived. I am glad to hear from you and my dear father. I hope we can keep up the correspondence you so kindly began. I will certainly do my part.

I am still in North Alabama, now staying with my uncle, Dr. Clarke. He recently lost his wife and has five little children, so my hands are full making their clothes and keeping house. I expect to stay until October. I receive letters from home every week, and each urges me to come. I must go on October 1. They want me earlier, but summer and early fall are very unhealthy there.

Uncle John is anxious for me to live with him. He is good and kind and does everything to make me happy and comfortable. He gives me anything I need, anticipates my wishes, and treats me like his own child. I love him dearly, but Brother Tommie has always done so much for me and wants me to live with him, so I will go in October. I love his children more than anything; they feel like my own. He has two beautiful boys, Russell and Eugene. Brother Tommie has two cows and little else. Brother Bobbie is with Brother Frank in the army.

Dear Mary lives two miles from here. Her health is very bad again. She looks so badly and weighs only eighty-four pounds. I weigh 125, and my health is very good.

Dear cousin, I wish I could go to Texas and see you all. It would delight me. Perhaps someday I shall have that pleasure. I am glad Papa likes Texas so much and hope he can find work there. I wish my brother would move there too. I hope Papa will stay with you and Aunt Cynthia. He would be much happier with you.

Cousin Lizzie, please send pictures of yourself, the family, and Aunt Cynthia's family. I would be so glad to have them. Write often and tell me all about my relatives. Ask some of my cousins to write. Tell Papa I am anxiously awaiting an answer to my letter.

Give him and Uncle an abundance of love, and also your mother, sisters, brothers, and Aunt Cynthia's family. Much love to you, from your unknown but loving cousin, Lummie.

### Lizzie Graves to Lizzie: sewing, emigration, and Valentines, undated fragment

[Page 30 labels this an incomplete, undated letter from Lizzie Graves. The surviving text reads from page 32 into page 31.]

…with all my time. During the last month I have made between twenty-five and thirty yards of cloth into clothing for Papa and my brothers.

Papa and Brother Joe went to Snowdoun today, about sixteen miles from here, to visit a friend and attend the Episcopal church. Joe did not go to Mobile as expected when I last wrote. I think he will leave tomorrow; where he will go afterward, he does not know himself. A company of emigrants is to leave Mobile for Brazil between the fifteenth and twentieth of this month, March. He wants to join them. If he cannot, he says he will settle in Texas.

I hope he fails in that attempt, because I am strongly opposed to his emigrating. I would much rather he return to his native state and share the fate of his own people than go with strangers to a strange country.

We are all well. Brother Doug has got rid of his chills. Charlie had one a few days ago, but I think it came from getting very wet. The river has been high for some time, and it has rained almost every day. I think Papa has planted about fifty acres of corn; I do not know how much more he will plant. We have [number unclear] hundred acres to cultivate.

The little boys dread cotton-picking time. Charlie says he would rather work in the harvest field than pick cotton, though he has little experience and may change his mind after trying it.

I suppose you could not afford to go and let Parson Bone make a fool of you—well, not quite, but nearly! During that last war, I reckon Mr. Reeves noticed nothing more interesting or instructive than the destruction of crops, though he also had opportunities to study human life.

I hope you enjoyed that Valentine. Write and tell me how it was received and how you managed to read it. Ellen Douglas has a Flora, but I do not know whether its emblems match mine. I am not sure I have a perfect copy of that document, but if you cannot make it out I can send as much of the translation as I have.

I received no Valentines. We are among strangers, so of course I expected none. You said you might send copies of original Valentines after they had been answered. I would enjoy them very much. Everything is so dull here except now and then when letters from friends bring a bright moment. I dream of seeing my friends but awake disappointed and must be content with letters for now.

I wrote to Sue Abbott recently and have had no reply. Lizzie, do not wait for me before writing. I wrote to Mollie not long ago and will write again soon. Your true friend, Lizzie Graves.

## Pages 33-35
### Lizzie Graves to Lizzie: family illness and hoped-for schooling, Saturday the nineteenth

[Page 33 identifies the writer and recipient and suggests Mobile, Alabama; the surviving letter supplies neither a full date nor a place.]

Saturday the nineteenth. Lizzie,

I expected to write much more, but circumstances prevented it. Grandma became very sick the night before last and was sick yesterday, although she is up this morning. Brother Joe had a chill the night before last. I hope the cold weather will bring an end to the chills.

I only just learned that someone from the family is going to town today, so I must close hurriedly. We expect visitors this winter. Aunt Jane—Jennie's mother—and her family will come in a few weeks. Her husband and two daughters with their families have gone to California. Uncle J. has gone to look at the country. If he likes it, he will move Aunt Jane and the children there. They will stay with us until he returns or sends for them.

We will have two young lady cousins and enough little children to keep us busy nursing. It will be hard to get used to the noise, since we have lived so quietly. The boys are in the fields most of the time, leaving Grandma and me alone.

I very much want to attend school this winter but will not be able to. I want Papa to send the little boys if possible; they are growing into big boys.

Lizzie, I have everything ready for riding except a new riding habit [outfit]. I have a new saddle, new gloves, and a little pony that will kneel for me to mount. The trouble is that I have few places to go, no one to ride with during the working season, and much work to do at home.

Brother D. is in a hurry, so I must close. Write soon. Papa's eyes are worsening and trouble me too [the final words are unclear]. I have not been sick this year; I am still fat and flourishing.

Yours with love, Lizzie Graves.

## Pages 36-38
### Lizzie Graves to Lizzie: adjusting to Alabama, March 10, 1867

[Page 36 identifies the home as Mosshead or Buttonwood Hall and the post-office location as Prairie, Alabama.]

Prairie, Alabama, Sunday evening, March 10, 1867. Dear Lizzie,

Your letter of February 16 and 17 reached me the day before yesterday. You cannot know how glad I was to receive it. Answers take so long that I sometimes think rather harshly of my friends for not writing. It seems only you and Mollie will write to me. This is my first letter from you, and I have had only one from Mollie. You say you wrote before, but I did not receive it.

I have nothing especially interesting to tell you and hardly know where to start, so I will begin with the least interesting but most important subject to myself: me. I am settled in this lonely, quiet house. I cannot quite call it home, though my thoughts naturally return to it. I have spent most of today reading and thinking. It has rained for nearly a week; because of this morning's rain we decided not to go to church.

Do not think I am discontented or unhappy with my new home, though I would much rather live in Texas and sometimes feel very sad about leaving. I am determined to be content wherever my lot is cast. Papa is doing the best he can for us. With our circumstances, and Grandma living here alone, it would be unkind for him to remain in Texas. Her only other son lives about fifteen miles away and has a large family to support. Papa thought moving here would also give us better opportunities for schooling.

I am sorry things have turned out differently. None of us can attend school now. I expected private lessons from a cousin in town, but she became very ill last summer and has never fully recovered. She also expects to give up housekeeping and is too unsettled. Apart from high board and tuition, living in town is too expensive for me.

I am trying to do all my own sewing this year. You know Papa hired much of it done last year, but I think I can manage with perseverance. [The continuation in the margin is partly unclear.]

Our present home is called Mosshead or Buttonwood Hall. I am sorry Laura is going so far from you; remember me kindly when you see her. Give my kindest regards to Grandfather and Mother and to the other relatives [the closing line is faint]. Tell Mollie to write often and kindly remember me to all. [Remaining marginal words are indistinct.]

Your friend, [Lizzie Graves, identified by the accompanying label].

## Pages 39-43
### Lizzie Graves to Lizzie: three deaths and a Sunday-school class, September 16, 1868

[Page 39 labels the date September 10; the letter heading on page 40 reads September 16. The difference is retained.]

Mosshead, Alabama, September 16, 1868. My dear Lizzie,

After such a long silence, I will try to talk with you through my pen. What caused it? Am I to blame? If so, I am sorry. But did I not answer your last letter? It has been so long that I cannot remember. I received one while Papa was sick and had little time to write. I have forgotten whether I replied. I have read it over but cannot tell. I have waited for a reply that never comes, so I will see whether this scribbling can draw a few words from you.

I feel I have much to say, but my mind wanders so much that I cannot put it together clearly. I will begin with the family's health. There has been much illness in the community, but we have been blessed. Papa had one severe attack and recovered before the sickest part of the season. Charlie had a slight fever for a few days. Apart from those cases, our immediate family escaped.

Cousins E. and D. Waddell came to visit and both became very sick for about two weeks. They were taken to town as soon as they could travel and have remained unwell. For about a week after they left, I spent all but two evenings visiting the sick. Brothers J. and D. have gone tonight to sit with a sick neighbor.

About a month ago I saw something I had never seen before: three bodies together, all to be buried in one family graveyard. Cousin M. W. and her infant were in one coffin, and Mrs. G.'s little girl in another. The coffins stood close together with the lids open, so all three could be seen while the preacher stood at their heads and performed the last sad rites. Yet they were a happy three: Cousin M. was a devout Christian, and the two little ones had never known sin.

Cousin M. left eight children, six girls and two boys. The eldest girl is in her fifteenth year. The eldest boy, about sixteen, had gone to Virginia for school a few days before his mother's death. How sad that the first news from home would tell him that the mother he left well had died! Poor Carrie wondered whether her mother mentioned her in her last hours. Her uncle said that she spoke neither of death nor of her children, but left an example for them. Her warning was short, yet we believe she was prepared.

I am teaching seven boys in Sunday school. The oldest is about fifteen. I am becoming deeply interested in them, but what a responsibility! What I teach will help shape their lives, bringing them toward Christ or taking them farther away. “He that does not gather with me scatters abroad.”

The eldest had received very little teaching. On his first Sunday I told him his next lesson. He said he had no book. I asked whether he had one at home; his family had no Bible. I gave him one, and he promised to read it. I know three or four more boys who need teaching and hope to gather them in soon.

I found some little girls who did not know who made them or anything about the Bible. I became interested in them and asked them to join my class. They agreed and now attend regularly when well. I could not teach them myself, so I found a teacher for them.

Three of us Sunday-school teachers are inexperienced: Miss Mattie B., Mr. C., and myself. We are helping each other as much as possible. Mr. C.'s girls are about as advanced as my boys, and we are encouraging friendly competition. We use no book but the Bible. Our lessons are historical. We first taught Christ's birth, ministry, and death, then went back to Abraham. We are now studying the patriarchs, from Abraham to David, drawing a practical lesson from each passage.

[Marginal additions:] I will close, trusting God to deliver these few words. [Some intervening words are unclear.] Does my letter not deserve an answer? Love to Grandma and all the family. Write soon.

## Pages 44-48
### Lizzie Graves to Lizzie: sickness, fruit, church, and an evening walk, September 30, 1867

[Page 44 is an identifying label. Some ink on pages 45-47 is faint.]

Mosshead, Alabama, Monday, September 30, 1867. My dear Lizzie,

Your kind letter of August 31 and September 1 reached me about a week ago. Please do not blame me for not answering sooner. I had the opportunity but wanted to write to you and Mollie together so that you might hear from each other's letters.

You must think me a great complainer. I have had reason: complaining seems the only way I get letters! You and Amanda Roberts have been my most punctual correspondents. You have been very good about forgiving me for keeping your letter so long unanswered; sickness is my excuse. I do not think my complaints unreasonable. I forgive my correspondents when they have a good reason. Oddly, none has complained about me. I suppose I do not deserve it.

It seems hard to have had so much sickness. Last week we were all able to be up at once, but that did not last. Everybody had to be sick, so Frank and I had chills. I have not had another, though I feel bad every other day. Frank still has them; he had one last night and has a high fever now. He has been sick much of the summer, and I fear it will set him back in school. Brother Doug has a chill [continuing on page 46] every third day, though otherwise the family is well. Doug is going to school in town but cannot learn much now. He is about four miles away and stays with a cousin until the cotton is gathered. He comes home about once a week.

I want good peaches so badly. I have had none since leaving home. I thought Alabama would be a fruit country, but find nothing here except a few bitter apples, not even as good as wild peaches. Grandma has no orchard now. Her fine apple and pear orchard was destroyed a few years ago by a tornado that blew down the trees.

She has a few fig trees. I enjoyed the figs while they lasted and wished you could share them, while I shared your peaches. There is little wild fruit: a few plums and muscadines, but I got none. Brushy, November 13, 1864. Dear George,

Forgive my delay; we have been very busy. This morning Sue and I went to Liberty to hear Mrs. Justin's funeral sermon. It was to have been preached at the Colony last Sunday, but Mr. Haynes was sick. Mr. Haynes gave a long, tedious sermon from the last clause of Hebrews 11:4. He spoke for fully two hours, much of it about the depreciation of currency. I do not mean the “old” and “new issue,” but the money itself. The “old issue” was well represented today and, judging by the display, is not likely to be repudiated soon. Em and Sis were there. I barely had time to speak to them. I wish you could have seen everyone.

Things are much as when you were here: much work and little time for visiting. We helped Aunt Bettie get ready to move. She had not prepared the thread for me to weave, but got some jeans cloth from Ellen, and we made the little boys' coats. She left on the morning of the 10th, taking Betsy and [Perkins?]. She left the spinach and her few cows. I think her uncle means to come back to tend them.

Father bought a sow and five young pigs from her for ten dollars in specie [coin], so I think we have meat secured for another year, if we can raise corn to feed them. Father reserved twenty acres for corn and Hungarian grass. Laura told you Gibson would repair the fence, but she was mistaken. That was not part of the bargain. He was only to raise the wheat, cut and thresh it, and put a fourth in the granary. How the old fence will ever be repaired I cannot say, but I hope it will all come right.

The well holds up well. About a dozen horses and mules are watered from it two or three times a day. It gets quite low but scarcely muddy, and by the next watering it is full again.

I would feel much better if I had any hope of the war ending soon. From the newspapers' tone, we should not expect peace for four more years. Old Abe is probably elected, and [Chidley?] thinks it best. He says that if McClellan is elected, he will offer terms some states will accept, and then we are ruined. So he seems to favor four more years of war.

Last Monday evening a cold north wind blew, and we were all around the fire when we heard “Come out!” We looked out, and there he was. Chidley was nearly frozen, but a warm fire soon got him talking. He and Mr. [G—?], a soldier with him, kept us laughing almost the whole visit.

I was very glad to see Chidley. You and Joda had talked so much about him that he seemed an old acquaintance. I never saw anyone more eager to get home. From your sending that shirt home, I suppose Joda must have drawn some for you. Draw everything you can; it is all the pay you can get. Mr. [Graves?] says the beans you sent will not come up because they have been scalded.

Henry and Ellen went home on the 8th, well and hearty. We have not heard from Warren but expect him and Beck down. Griffin, or someone else, must have a particular spite against Warren. Unless he got his furlough extended, his stay at home will be short.

We recently received Dock [Hyneman?]'s letter of October 10. He had just received Father's letter and the socks we sent by Ed [Dacham?]. He was very glad of them, as he needed them. He belongs to Company G, Bates's regiment, stationed at Velasco, and expects to stay there this winter. He ended his letter by saying, “I am a peace man, let it come as it may.”

Ed Dickson is now at Tyler guarding prisoners. He deserves credit for taking the trouble to send the socks on to Dock. Many soldiers would have kept them in these scarce times.

Mr. M— seems inclined to stay in the service. In his last letter to Sister, he told her to buy two more wagons, a horse wagon and an ox wagon, and join him in the spring. Sister is very pleased; she has long wanted to leave here. She thinks she can rent a house in San Antonio and send the children to school.

Jesse arrived yesterday. He will stay only a few days, because he is going to San Antonio with a load of wool as soon as he returns. Henry and Ellen reached home safely. Warren has gone home. They say that after she died, her eyes popped out of her head. While they were burying her, her little brother suddenly became ill and died within two hours. He had had a chill or two before, but that morning he got up, helped catch a chicken, and ate a hearty breakfast. It is a strange disease: they did not complain of any pain.

Mr. Tilly, Nancy Coffman's husband, has been sick a long time. Last Sunday morning, a small red spot appeared on his arm and spread rapidly. The arm swelled all over, gangrene set in, and he died Monday. I have heard of other sudden deaths, all thought to be the same disease in different forms.

A. Bell is now suffering severely from his wound. His shoulder is greatly swollen, with an area six inches across in which, they say, the ball can be felt moving back and forth. They think it can be removed, but the doctor will not do it until the weather gets cooler.

Wednesday morning, the 7th, 10 o'clock. Mr. L. Evans called and left the books you sent and a letter from Joda. You had better believe I was glad to see someone from among the boys. He came riding up through the rain; it had been falling nearly all morning. He was in such a hurry that he stayed no more than fifteen minutes. We wanted him to stay for dinner, but he wanted to reach [place damaged] for it. I suppose they all feel that way when they get so close to home. He will arrive tonight. He says he left Sabine on the 31st, making seven days on the road tonight. Isn't that pretty good traveling?

Mr. Evans says you told him you would not come home before Christmas. He also says you could get leave just as he did. You know more about it than I do, but if you think well of it, I would rather you came now. Who knows? Perhaps the furlough business will end altogether, or you will have to go to Louisiana or somewhere else. Thirty days is short, but for someone who can stay only a short time, it would do some good at home. I am glad old Magruder is gone.

Meetings are about all we have going on, and every doctrine is making itself heard. You ask whether the good feeling continues. In August it was announced that there would be a protracted meeting at the next appointment, with several eminent preachers expected. The Methodists then arranged a meeting in Mr. Butcher's neighborhood at the same time. That did not seem very charitable. Parson Hill says that if he and his neighbor cannot agree, he should just leave him alone. The union meetings seem to have been forgotten.

Our meeting began on the Thursday before the first Sunday. Attendance was small until Sunday, but the effort to draw people away failed: there was a crowd, with seats in the grove. Only the Methodists are so hostile; the Presbyterians seem less afraid of the truth [the writer's opinion]. Bro. Jones was the only visiting preacher and did most of the preaching. We asked Parson Austin to preach Sunday evening. His text was “It is finished,” and he gave a good talk. There were no conversions, but four joined: Milton Rawlins and his wife, Sarah Selby, and Lizzie Bennett. The meeting closed Monday. Jones preached his best sermon, on unity, to a full house. I hope it made a good impression.

Parson Austin did not attend regularly but heard several good sermons. In his own words: “I tell my brethren that they”—the Christians—“have the bulge on us and we had better let them alone.” That is an odd expression. I do not know why it was done, but another protracted meeting was appointed, beginning Friday night before the first Sunday in October. Bro. Goodman said several preachers would be there whom we had never heard before. How I wish you could be with us then. I know you would enjoy it.

Parson Austin visited Sis Gibson last Sunday and went Saturday night. Don't you think that rather early? His wife had been dead only a little over two weeks.

Father has just returned from the [unclear]. Mr. Ringett went down to get an auger to bore for water. He brings two pieces of news. One is that Fayette and Jim have either been captured or killed: a letter says the whole brigade was captured. F. and J. have a hard time of it. The other is that Mary Morgan is to marry Mr. Phips tonight. And so they go.

You ask whether I would like some of your cobbler. It would be a treat indeed; I have tasted only one this year. But I would gladly eat supper with you some night even if it were only bread and water.

I saw Walk Hatter the other day. He looks about as he used to. He was a prisoner for four months and says he fared well in New Orleans. The soldiers he spoke with said they would fight us for ten years if necessary to beat us. They said old Abe gave them plenty to eat and wear, and that was all they cared about.

You say I can write long letters now. I have done so this time, but I fear it will not be interesting to you. Lizzie.
