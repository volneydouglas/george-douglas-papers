## 09-002 — Rebecca Goodloe to her daughter Mary, April 18, 1852

Rutherford County, Tennessee, April 18, 1852. Dear daughter,
Your letter and Rebecca’s, dated October 18, 1851, arrived in good time. I would have answered, but expected another once Mr. Andrews reached you. I have waited all this time without receiving one.
You ask me to write often. I know how glad I am to receive a letter, and it warms my heart to hear from you all. I am glad Susanna was baptized. I hope she will do good, be generous and ready to share, building a foundation for eternal life, as the passage in Timothy says.
My sister Betsey died on January 26. She suffered greatly and was [[“tapt” seven times?—word uncertain]]. For nine days she lived on a little snow and melted snow; it was all she wanted. She was ready to die. Heavy snow fell on January 18. I visited whenever I could and was with her when she died.
John Cock told me that everyone was well except Mr. Douglass. I am sorry he is ill and hope he can care for himself and recover. I do not know how you manage where everything is so scarce and expensive. Please tell me whether the things I sent with Mr. Andrews were damaged during [[“cashing,” meaning uncertain]], and whether the peafowl feathers arrived safely.

<a id="s09p3"></a>

## 09-003 — Weaving, gardening, household work, and neighborhood news

Give my sincere regards to Mr. and Mrs. Andrew for their kindness and generosity. I hope they like their new country, prosper in this life, and find happiness in the next. Rebecca asks what became of all the girls who married after she left. I cannot tell her everything; I think some would have been better off single. Isabella Hill moved to the western district. She had a daughter, Rhoda Louisa, and I heard her health was poor when she left.
Now for my own affairs. Thank God, I am reasonably healthy. I can get about and manage business, knit, sew, card wool or cotton, and do some spinning on the little wheel. I bought a loom, and Mary has woven [[number unclear: “2” followed by another figure]] lengths of cloth this winter. That is better than having my weaving done elsewhere. I have been gardening: peas are up and supported; Irish potatoes and onions are growing; beans and okra are planted; and beets, cucumbers, peppers, and cabbage plants are growing. We have planted some corn and will plant cotton this week. Mary tends the crop and garden with Sam.
Judy has a baby boy named Jim, said to be three months old. She does the cooking and washing. Amanda is at Betsey Graham’s; I do not know whether I can spare her from the crop. Alfred arranged for William Arbuckil to take Tom for the year in return for food and clothing and to teach him to work efficiently. Bill is here, carrying water and watching the children. Enough about that.

<a id="s09p4"></a>

## 09-004 — Family marriages and moves; signed Mother Rebecca Goodloe

Cousin Betsey Adams, Billy Adams’s widow, has moved here and rented the [[Gonnatya?]] house from Mr. Hall. I let her boys farm some land. Her son Billy works at the sawmill and gristmill. Brother Jacob bought the mills from Ned Adam and is doing good business. He is busy as a bee, building a bridge. Everyone at home is well; Rebecca and Anda Alexander are well too.
Granville Jetton married Miss Elizabeth [[Conn?]]. Dick Jones married his third wife, Miss Mary, Elder McHenry’s daughter. Mary Waight has married Sam Rucker. Mary Goodloe remains helpless and was worse when I last heard. William Goodloe moved to Trenton, Gibson County, in the western district. Henry Goodloe’s daughters Altamira and Emily went along to spend the summer with relatives. James Goodloe married Miss Ann Neal of Gibson County and lives in Trenton.
Rebecca asked about Ned Adams and Cousin Betsey. They are well and doing better, although their little girl, one of the twins, has died. I cannot remember half the news Rebecca wants. Cousin Livve Posey and everyone in my family are well except Hannah, who is frail and has severe spells of illness. Betsey bought a house and lot in Woodbury with her own money and has the deed in her name.
Polly, just here Alfred arrived with your long-awaited letter. I read it with great satisfaction. I began this letter on Sunday, added to it yesterday, and now it is Tuesday. I was nearly finished when Alfred came. Julie is staying with Betsey in Woodbury. Your loving mother, Rebecca Goodloe. To Mary Douglass.

## 09-005 — Rebecca Goodloe to her granddaughter Susannah, April 21, 1852

Wednesday, April 21. Dear Susannah,
I was nearly done writing when your grateful letter arrived. It gave me great pleasure and comfort. Thank you, dear granddaughter. I hope you will always remember me as your grandmother. Your letter was very good; I did not know you could write so well. I am sorry your father is ill. I would almost urge him to try a water treatment if he knew how to use it, though he must judge for himself.
The remedy I describe for cramping colic is a blanket dipped in hot water and wrung out, wrapped around the person with more warm blankets or a featherbed, and heated rocks or boiled corn at the feet and shoulders to bring on sweating. I mention this for your mother, who used to suffer from colic.
Write again and tell me what everyone is doing. Give Rebecca my love and ask her to write as soon as this arrives. Tell the boys to write too. My love to all the family. I will try to write again; the others here must write for themselves. That is all I can do now. Your affectionate grandmother, Rebecca Goodloe. To Susannah Douglass.
Hannah was very glad to hear from you all.

Reading note: The water and heat treatments are reproduced solely as historical content, not present-day medical advice.

## 09-006 — S. J. Goodloe reports his mother’s death, June 18, 1852

Woodbury, Cannon County, Tennessee, June 18, 1852. Mr. N. L. Douglas,
Dear Sir, I understand Lewis Jetton wrote about Mother’s death, but did not describe the circumstances of that sudden and sorrowful event. My dear mother is gone. She died on May 6, nearly seventy years old. She faced life’s hardships with patience and Christian conduct, reaching a standard of integrity, morality, and faith that few attain. At last she rests peacefully in the grave, ready, in our faith, to rise to heaven in God’s eternal kingdom on the morning of resurrection.

<a id="s09p7"></a>

## 09-007 — The sudden collapse on May 6

On the morning of May 6, she got up apparently as well as usual and managed household business normally. Late that evening, while sewing trousers, she suddenly fell from her chair, as though paralyzed. [[Jena?]], a Black girl and the only other person in the room, helped her to bed. Mother called Julia when she fell, but Julia was occupied in another room and did not initially think it serious. Julia arrived about the time Mother reached the bed. When asked how she felt, Mother said she was very ill and seemed dazed. Her symptoms suggested what the writer called apoplexy. She soon seemed better, although at times her words showed lapses of memory. I had been—

<a id="s09p8"></a>

## 09-008 — Her final half-hour and those present

—away all day. I returned just before sunset, and Julia told me Mother was sick. At her bedside I asked what was wrong. She said her stomach was upset and her eyes and head hurt, though she felt better. I left the room; about ten minutes later Julia called that Mother was dying. I hurried back and saw that she was close to death. About half an hour later she was gone. She scarcely struggled and clasped my hand as though saying goodbye. She had not spoken since telling me she felt better.
The writer lists [[Jena?]], Hannah, and himself as those of the family present; some nearby neighbors were also there. Hannah grieves deeply and is in poor health. Julia, Hannah, and Alfred will stay on the farm—

<a id="s09p9"></a>

## 09-009 — S. J. Goodloe’s signature and the household’s plans

—until the harvest is gathered; then they will separate. Julia and Hannah will live with either Sister Rebecca or Betsey. Give my love to all the family. Yours as ever, S. J. Goodloe.
Postscript: Our relatives are generally well. S. J. G.

## 09-011 — Rebecca sends cloth and clothing to Polly, October 6, 1851

October 6, 1851. Dear Polly,
I hope everyone is well. Thank God, I am as well as usual. Hannah is still unwell; walking about and smoking her pipe are all she does.
News from the western district: Mr. Nance has a fever, while Lucy and four children have chills and fever. Cynthia and her whole family have been sick. Your brother Sam is attending law school in Lebanon. We have heard nothing from Mobile since last spring. My sister Betsey Woods is very ill with dropsy. Last Thursday four gallons of fluid were drained from her, relieving her suffering somewhat, but I do not expect her to recover. The Andrews family will bring you all the news.
I am sending cloth with Mr. Andrews for you to use as you think best. I hope Mr. Douglas and Warren will have coats if they wish. There are twenty-two yards of jeans fabric in the trunk. I intended the green for George’s coat and trousers. I could not get it all dyed. The weaver, Martha Ann [[goad?]] Adams, hurried to finish because John wanted to move to Arkansas. I suggest walnut bark and a little copperas to dye George’s trousers black, since the last piece is uneven in color. There are thirteen yards of dark brown and five of pale brown. I am also sending summer cloth for Jesse’s and Warren’s trousers, and eight and a half yards of plaid for the little girls’ winter dresses. There are yarn socks for Mr. Douglass, cotton stockings for you and Laura, little blue ones for Mary, and yarn to knit—

<a id="s09p12"></a>

## 09-012 — Seeds, quills, baptisms, and a marriage rumor

—stockings for the other children. I am sending Rebecca eight yards of calico. If that is not enough for her dress, she should give it to Susanna. I am sending cotton dresses for Susanna and Laura, and three and a half yards of blue checked calico for little Mary. If that will not make a dress, use it for something else. There are peafowl feathers, goose quills, sage, garden comfrey seed I grew myself, and a little starch.
I am sorry to burden Mr. Andrew, but he willingly does what he can for Mr. Douglass, whom he calls a great friend. Give my love to Jesse, Henry, and everyone else, and accept the same yourself. Your mother, Rebecca Goodloe.
We had a four- or five-day meeting at Riplecreek Meetinghouse. Brothers Trot and Corlee preached, as did [[Sanda?]] Jones. Fourteen were baptized; the list includes John Smith, [[Eveart and Houghs?]], two of Nathan Hale’s daughters, and [[Polenna?]] Jones. Emily Goodloe and Altamira had been baptized earlier. I cannot write any more now. Write as often as possible, and ask Rebecca and Susanna to write whenever they can send mail. I nearly forgot: I heard Sarah Barr is going to marry.
To Mary Douglass. The braid is to trim Susanna’s green sack jacket; put two rows around it.

## 09-013 — The Pleasures of Memory: an unsigned essay

The Pleasures of Memory
How often our thoughts return to the pleasures of earlier days, formed before we knew the cares of the world—memories that time cannot erase.
Life’s worries often weigh on us until we see nothing bright. Then a familiar sound or a clear, musical voice suddenly stirs our hearts and returns us to a treasured scene.
We remember loved ones at home, whose hands cared for us and whose guidance led us through life’s uneven paths.

<a id="s09p14"></a>

## 09-014 — Memories of home, prairie flowers, and streams

We remember home, that sacred place where we spent our happiest hours:
No matter how bleak the place,
How grand or humble the home,
Love keeps bonds that never die
And ties our hearts to home.

We remember its surroundings and the hours spent wandering the broad prairies gathering colorful flowers. That was then the height of our ambitions. We delighted in following the moss-covered banks of nearby streams, forgetting how quickly time passed.

<a id="s09p15"></a>

## 09-015 — Sunday-school memories and the comfort of remembrance

Think of the later pleasure of being old enough for Sunday school: the happiness of entering the room, gathering eagerly around the teacher, and learning from the Bible.
Children look into the teacher’s face with complete trust, and the lessons leave a lasting impression. Those scenes remain in my memory. I imagine that near the end of life we can still say:
There are moments in life we never forget,
Growing brighter as time slips away;
They add new delight to our happiest days,
And bring light to our loneliest day.

## 09-016 — Patriotic Song, bearing Emily Douglas’s name

Patriotic Song
Of all the lands in east or west,
I love my native country best.
It overflows with God’s good gifts.
Though neither gold nor gems are found,
People with noble hearts abound,
And eyes shine brightly with joy. [Repeat.]

Of all the tongues in east or west,
I love my native language best.
It may not be so smoothly spoken,
Or woven with Italian skill,
But when heart speaks to heart,
Its word is never broken. [Repeat.]

Of all the peoples east or west,
I love my countrymen the best,
A people of a noble spirit.
A sober mind, a generous heart,
Taught goodness, free from artifice,
They inherit [[from?]] their ancestors. [Repeat.]

I offer my hand to all the world,
But give my heart to my native land,
Seeking its good and glory.
I honor every nation’s name,
Respect its fortune and its fame,
But love the land that bore me. [Repeat.]

Emily Douglas.

## 09-018 — Miriam Stevenson reconnects with Polly from Columbus, October 24, 1855

Columbus, October 24, 1855. Mrs. Douglas, my dear old friend,
This evening I am finally doing what I have long wished: writing to you. Until recently I did not know your address. I have just returned from Tennessee, our old home, where I learned where you live. Mrs. Sam Davis passed along a message from her husband. I was delighted to hear that you are doing well and have good prospects. I wish you a long, happy life; no one cares more deeply about you and your family.
Polly—as I am used to calling you—we have both faced many troubles since we parted. I believe they work for our good if we love the Lord. I have learned that earthly things pass away. This is not my permanent home; I look toward the city built by God. My heart and treasure are there, and I have no reason to complain. I am healthy and weigh 144.
In Tennessee I saw your sisters, Mrs. Jones and Mrs. Nance. Doca was visiting and looks very thin, as does Julia. I did not see—

<a id="s09p19"></a>

## 09-019 — Changes in Tennessee and Miriam’s life after her husband’s death

—Rebecca or Betsey, because I could not conveniently visit, though I heard they were well. I saw many old neighbors and new people too. Mary Weather[[s?]], for instance, has seven daughters! Even the countryside seems strange; few people or things looked familiar.
Rebecca Alexander looks well, but Andy looks unwell. Uncle Jacob and Aunt Polly look much aged, although they worry as much as ever about worldly affairs. There are too many changes to recount. Sister Louisa wrote this morning that Virginia McKnight, formerly Johnson, died. She visited her mother on Sunday, became ill that night, and died on Thursday. Sister Lou lives comfortably and produces plenty for her needs. During a short stop in Murfreesboro, I saw Aunt Brown, Amanda Mosby, Dora Brown, and Dora Nance together, but could not stay to hear much news.
As for me, I still teach and try to be useful. I kept house for two and a half years—the happiest period of my life. After my husband died, I stopped keeping my own household. He had four children. His relatives took three, and I kept one: a sweet girl named Virginia, now almost ten.

<a id="s09p20"></a>

## 09-020 — Teaching, raising Virginia, and maintaining old friendships

My chief concern is to raise and educate her. She is advanced for her age. I have enough money to support us without working, but I prefer employment because it is good for health and happiness. For several years I have boarded at a hotel, so I have little to do besides attend school and church. We have a pleasant church building and meet every Sunday to worship according to the Bible.
Mr. Darnall, Elizabeth, and Eveline have recently had chills, but they live nearby and are doing well. Thomas Sherrill also lives here and is one of the best artists [[words obscured]]. If this letter reaches you, I will send an example of his work as a keepsake with my next letter. Please write a long reply soon. Remember me to anyone in the family who recalls me. Surely Mr. Douglass has not forgotten me; tell him I would be delighted to receive a letter. [[Unclear fragment.]]
A fond farewell. Yours sincerely, Miriam Stevenson.

## 09-021 — Miriam sends her portrait, March 29, 1856

Columbus, March 29, 1856. Dearest Mrs. Douglass,
After a long delay I gladly acknowledge your welcome letter. The cold, unpleasant winter made the mail irregular. Now that the weather is better, I will venture to send my portrait. I do not think it important to anyone, but I want to remain in your memory. Perhaps you have forgotten exactly how I look; if so, it will recall earlier days. Here I am in my forty-eighth year, weighing 145 and feeling as young as nineteen.
I am healthy except for occasional neuralgia, that fashionable complaint, which ties me in knots. I still teach, but think this may be my last term. I am tired of being confined. I would love to travel if I were braver, but water and steam make me very nervous.

<a id="s09p22"></a>

## 09-022 — An enduring friendship and news from Sister Lou

I am delighted that you and your children are well. They are dear to me because they are yours. I would love to see everyone, though I cannot promise I ever will. Neither time nor absence can erase the happy memories of our youth; they remain fresh places in my memory that distance cannot destroy.
Sister Lou wrote recently that your cousin Tompson Wright married Miss [[Bowen?]], and that Virginia McKnight, William’s wife, died. Everyone here is well and sends greetings. Tell Mr. Douglass I value his kindness and return his good wishes fourfold.
My love to my dear cousins James and Cynthie. I remember them fondly and hope they remember me. Write soon. My best wishes go with you and your family. Farewell, dear friend. May the Lord protect you through life, comfort you in death, and receive you in heaven. Your friend, Miriam Stevenson.

## 09-024 — Miriam describes the portrait’s dress and hairstyle, July 6, 1856

Columbus, July 6, 1856. My dear friend Mrs. Douglas,
Your letter of May 26 arrived safely and was warmly welcomed. I was amused that you thought there might have been a mistake with the picture. I assure you it is me! I wore a mohair headdress with three curls on each side. My dress was rich blue-and-brown brocade, but the colors did not show in the picture.
I am glad to hear of your health and prospects. I pray you have a long, useful, happy life, and that I may see you again. What a pleasant thought! But we must not presume upon the future; only the present is ours. Remembering our happy hours together saddens me because they cannot return. Still, we should not let memories of the past darken the future.
I am fairly well and will soon spend two months in the country. The hot season is unpleasant here—

<a id="s09p25"></a>

## 09-025 — Country air and religious fellowship

—and it will be lovely to breathe the country air. I long to go. After ten months of being closely confined, I am free again.
I have just returned from church and will finish the letter. Our preacher was sick, so there was no sermon, but we observe our worship practices whether a preacher is present or not. We never neglect that. If everyone valued their religious freedom, Christians would gather to sing, praise, and give thanks, rejoicing in the Lord’s commands.
For me, this is life’s greatest pleasure. I owe much to God for his kindness, especially since I moved south, where circumstances have favored my Christian life. You know that earlier I was persecuted, shunned, and mocked for my beliefs and practices. Yet I have always rejoiced—and still do—that I may share in the suffering of the one who suffered so much for me.

<a id="s09p26"></a>

## 09-026 — Miriam responds to Mary’s discouragement

You sounded discouraged in your last letter. I fear you give too much weight to worldly troubles. You wrote that the days of your pilgrimage had been few and unhappy. Do not let such thoughts intrude. You have, I believe, a very kind husband and children who have never disgraced you. Reflect again, and you will find countless reasons for gratitude instead of complaint.
I fear you may not have surrendered fully to God’s guidance. Without that, I believe, you cannot expect peace. Do not take this to mean I consider you a great sinner—no! I mean you must belong to Christ’s body. Attend to that, my friend, and secure your place in heaven.
Please write again; your letters give me great pleasure. I hope to send you a present when the opportunity comes. Give Mr. Douglass my affection [[the original modifier is uncertain]], respect, and esteem, and remember me to the children who recall me.

<a id="s09p27"></a>

## 09-027 — Miriam’s closing greeting to James and Cynthia

Tell Cousin James and Cynthia that I care deeply about their well-being. I hope they live like Zachariah and Elizabeth. May knowledge of the truth bring increasing peace. Yours in hope, M. Stevenson.

## 09-028 — W. Douglas to his sister Rebecca, June 3, 1856

Wardville, June 3, 1856. Dear Sister,
I was delighted to receive your letter of May 20. At first I was a little surprised—then surprised at my own surprise. What could be more natural than for us to think of one another and write often? It is surprising we have not done so before. I could never forget you or love you less as time and circumstances change. Every memory of my past is inseparably connected with you.
As far back as I can remember, you were my constant and almost only childhood playmate. Through boyhood, youth, and adulthood, you remained my companion. How could we ever lose our warm, lasting affection?
Other relationships may form and other friendships fade, but ours is woven into our lives—an affection [[the following image about roots and clay is uncertain]].
I am well. There is little else to report about myself, since nothing happens that would interest you—

<a id="s09p29"></a>

## 09-029 — Why he wants to leave Texas for California

—in these hard times. Spring was very wet, but now we have had three dry weeks and badly need rain. The corn looks fairly promising; wheat is doing poorly here.
Henry’s letter came with yours. He was well, but business is slow for him too. Like me, he seems uncertain about his future.
You sound horrified by my idea of going to California. I do not think you need to be. Other men—better men than your brother—are there or going there. Every intelligent person I have spoken to who has seen California speaks highly of it. Many urge me to go; some are returning with their families.
I cannot get beyond a hand-to-mouth existence in Texas without being extremely strict in business and spending. Neither suits my nature or my outlook. Why should I feel so tied to Texas when I am already effectively and hopelessly separated from you and everyone else who loves me?
If there is a better place, why not find it? Why not enjoy the best this little world offers, when even its best is poor enough? I do not expect sudden—

Reading note: “Your brother” explains the unusual phrase “men, & better than yours”; it is a contextual interpretation, not an added name. His California journey remains a plan in this letter.

<a id="s09p30"></a>

## 09-030 — Preparations for California and a proposed visit

—wealth from moving, but I think I can do better there. I have considered it for a long time, and it is worth going even if only to discover that I was mistaken. You can be fairly certain I will go to California in the spring, if I live. I have already begun making arrangements.
District Court opens here Monday, and I must testify in a land case. If nothing intervenes, I will visit immediately afterward, though business may prevent it. Uncle Lewis and the others were well when I last heard. My regards to Norvell and all friends who ask after me.
Truly your brother, W. Douglas. To Rebecca Winnifrid.
Write often. Your letters will always give me fresh pleasure. W. D.

## 09-031 — Condolences on George’s father’s death, July 9, probably 1874

Corinth, Mississippi, July 9, [[1874?]]. Cousin George,
I have received your June 23 letter with the sad news of your father’s death. We were very sorry, though at his advanced age it was to be expected in the natural course of life. Those of us still living are well and doing well. Sister Marthah died last August. [[Ma?]] has been healthier this year than for several years.
About the [[unclear financial matter]], I never meant any of you to worry. I thought I had written that several times. Give the amount to Aunt Polly and Cousin Lizzie; if that does not settle matters, write again. We have a good crop. I have been hoeing cotton today and feel little like writing, which explains this short letter and poor handwriting.
Please write a long letter describing the country, its changes, the railroad routes, and land prices. Love to everyone. Yours, E. M. Hyneman.

May 31, probably 1863. The boat is in sight, ten or fifteen miles away, so I will try to finish before it arrives. Boats are welcome as our link with the outside world.
A flag-of-truce boat recently went out to the blockading ships to learn what had happened to a man named Taylor. Ill treatment by secessionists had driven him to the Yankees, and he was wounded in the lighthouse fight. His wife, who lives about twenty-five miles upriver, requested the inquiry. If his fate was learned, it has not been announced.
The boat brought recent Northern newspapers. I read the New York Times yesterday. It firmly supports the Union as it was and the Constitution as it is, offers the rebels no peace, and calls on patriotic men to keep fighting until the rebellion is suppressed. If two more years will not finish it, the next administration may pursue it with renewed vigor.
Its account of the recent Hooker–Lee battle gives a Union loss figure that is now damaged, and Confederate losses of roughly 20,000–25,000 [[part of the range is damaged]]. It claims 2,500 prisoners and seventeen artillery pieces. It says a sudden rise in the Rappahannock cut rail communication with the rear for twelve [[unit lost]], and that Confederate troops did not interfere with the retreat. It also prints Commodore Porter’s report on the capture of Grand Gulf [[date damaged]].

## 09-100 — George’s response to Laura: “I write every thing about myself”

Porter says he has the gateway to Vicksburg. In his dispatch, he says the natural defenses at Grand Gulf are stronger than Vicksburg’s and that completed fortifications would have made the place impossible for him to take. ## 09-101 — George in Houston: uncertainty about orders, March 19, 1865

Houston, March 19, 1865. Sister Lizzie,
I am writing again from here with little idea how long we may remain. We have no reliable information about our next movements, though most think we will go east of the Mississippi this spring. Few want to go, I assure you, but few would refuse if ordered.
There is little important news. A rumor a few days ago said Sherman had been defeated in battle and Beauregard killed. The newspapers say nothing about it. The last reliable reports said Sherman and Hardee were racing for Charlotte, with Sherman likely to arrive first.
There will clearly be active campaigning there this spring. Unless Yankees come this way, someone from this department will surely have to go east of the Mississippi. Either an attack on Galveston is feared, or coastal troops will be relieved and sent into the field. The old troops from Louisiana and Arkansas are all coming this way.

<a id="s09p102"></a>

## 09-102 — Troop movements, coffee, and a sermon without politics

Henry Orr wrote on the 13th that they expected to move to the Brazos country in a few days: forage around Waverly was exhausted. He asked me to reply to Hempstead, where DeBray’s regiment already is. Tom wrote from Tyler on the 10th that his old regiment was marching to Hempstead. I understand Walker’s old division and Green’s old brigade have orders too. To me, this suggests fear of an attack on the coast, or starvation in Louisiana and Arkansas.
We are healthy and live fairly well. We pooled our money and bought a sack of flour and thirty pounds of coffee, so our household has coffee and biscuits. That brings our coffee purchases since arriving here to about forty-five pounds.
Confederate money has fallen to “27” again [[the basis is not stated]] and circulates poorly. Coin is scarcer too. Goods are generally cheaper because money is scarce. Blockade running continues at Galveston and keeps the market supplied. Cotton carding tools sold at auction for 1.60 a pair when bought by the dozen pairs.
Today I heard the old Presbyterian preacher give an excellent sermon without mentioning the war, the Confederacy, Jeff, Abe or any other political dignitary.
As ever, your brother, George.

## 09-108 — George mourns his sister, March 10, 1865

Houston, Texas, March 10, 1865. To everyone at home,
I was deeply saddened to hear of my sister’s death from Captain Evans, who arrived a few days ago. I never expected such sad news. It came unexpectedly, but was all too true. We can only weep for those who have died, though our sorrow cannot bring them back. The evidence they left of their hope of immortality comforts those left behind.
It gives me greater happiness to know that she died hoping for heaven and everlasting joy than if she had been crowned the richest queen on earth. We mourn her loss, but [[a damaged passage refers to her gain]]. It seems hard that she should be taken from her young family. Yet Providence’s ways are best, and we must submit without complaint.
It is terribly sad for the children to lose their protector so early, and the care and love only a mother can give, leaving them almost on their own in the world for a time. Sympathy for them deepens our sorrow. We must do everything we can for them, separated by fate or fortune from their only parent.
More than ever I feel needed at home, but my chances of returning soon seem smaller and smaller. As we go on, the future grows darker, with [[not one?]] ray of light ahead.

<a id="s09p109"></a>

## 09-109 — “Any way almost would suit me now”: George longs for the war to end

There are many rumors that we will be ordered across the Mississippi, or to Louisiana. I am fairly sure we will start east somewhere by the beginning of next month. If sent across the river, an individual would have little chance of remaining here, because transfers would not be allowed. Almost all the men oppose such a move, but you know what orders mean. It is reported that Lee has ordered 30,000 troops east of the Mississippi. If true, I fear the worst. I will try not to worry more than I can help; I only wish my service were over.
Our only news comes through the blockade. Sherman is occupying coastal cities almost [[without?]] opposition, while [[Lee?]] is concentrating his forces. I expect this year’s campaigning to take place mostly in the field. Perhaps it will bring the war to an end, through subjugation if nothing else. Almost any ending would suit me now.
Today has been a day of fasting, and everything has been quiet. I do not attend their war meetings or sermons.
The country is ready to accept any kind of government now and submit to anything: its spirit is broken. It is late and cold, so I will close.
With love to all, affectionately, George Douglas.

## 09-111 — George to Laura: dry weather and the stranded Clifton, March 11, 1864

Sabine Pass, March 11, 1864. Letter 60. Sister Laura,
This week’s mail brought your letter of February 21. It found us all doing well. You have had cold weather too; from what I hear, the winter has been fairly severe everywhere.
It remains very dry. Without rain soon, our cistern water will run out, and the well water is poor. Life around Sabine Pass goes on as usual, with little to attract attention. The pleasant weather only increases the common garrison disease of laziness.
Two men in our company are ill, one at Beaumont and one here. Both have pneumonia, one following measles, the other an ordinary case. From what I can learn, neither is in much danger.
The Clifton has not left yet. She tried about two weeks ago but ran aground on an oyster reef at high tide. She lay tilted for a day and a night, opening a leak in her bottom. It is not bad enough to keep her in port, and she will try again when conditions are favorable.

<a id="s09p112"></a>

## 09-112 — George questions newspaper victories and growing rumors

Our news from outside is scanty: only what the newspapers supply. Naturally, they give us the favorable version, even when it is unreliable. Lying has become so fashionable that nearly every source of information is full of it. Some people seem so practiced and accustomed to it that they can lie without knowing it.
Sherman has supposedly been badly beaten in Mississippi twice! The first time, he certainly ran fast—but unfortunately for the South, the Rebels were ahead of him. If his latest retreat was like the first, where is Polk now?
The farther a story travels, the bigger it becomes. Since Vicksburg fell, news must come by a roundabout route and grows accordingly. We cannot trust details from east of the Mississippi, only the broad outline—and that looks rather dark.
Your letters suggest some strange ideas have reached the upper country. Yankees and Rebels must be on better terms farther west if schooners can pass in and out freely with only a few blank shots fired at them. Here they shoot to hurt. When nothing more entertaining offers itself, the Yankees shell our sentries on the beach.

<a id="s09p113"></a>

## 09-113 — War weariness and teasing Laura about marriage

Several Yankees came ashore around dark the other evening. Our cavalry failed to capture them because they could not find them in the night. The people in your area seem thoroughly beaten down, even more than the soldiers—or perhaps they speak more freely. Maybe we are brave and maybe not; who knows?
There is less talk about military affairs now. Everyone seems indifferent. Boasting about Confederates and belittling Yankees is an old, worn-out song, scarcely hummed anymore. No one explains how women and children will live when their present supplies are gone, or seems greatly interested. Still, this quiet may last only a few weeks before everything becomes turmoil again.
And people still marry! I was surprised by Mary [[Patton’s?]] marriage, thinking her too young to leave her mother. I know Em Chapman only by reputation, and barely that. I believe she is Lizzie’s cousin; I wonder whether Lizzie will do the same. White people are unpredictable, and so are women.
Do not marry some gentleman of the “Bush” merely because the opportunity comes. Better call back “[[Bowle?]]” first, though he is old and rather redheaded.

## 09-114 — Jayhawkers, writing paper, and beef buried with military honors

A raid by Jayhawkers from the Louisiana swamps toward Niblett’s has attracted some notice. I understand Green’s brigade has been sent to deal with them. There is quite a group beyond Calcasieu Lake, causing trouble in that area. They are reportedly supported by Banks—but nowadays every kind of wrongdoing is blamed on Yankees.
I have enough paper for six months, so do not worry about supplying me. I am grateful to Sister for her present but will not need it for a while. Perhaps the changing fortunes of war will let me visit home and collect it when I need it. Tell her I will try to write this month. I received Mary’s letter recently. I must tell her she is still very modest and should outgrow that.
Gilbert, one of our company, has been released from the guardhouse. He is the man who left [[Millican?]] sick and went home last fall.
We still have enough to eat, but it is poor food. Some troops on Galveston Island have gone without beef for a week. The last they received was so bad that they buried it with military honors and refused any more because it was no better.
Affectionately, George.

## 09-115 — George travels down the Brazos, April 11, 1865

Houston, April 11, 1865. Sister Laura,
I promised a longer letter this week, so I must keep my promise. On Wednesday morning I started for Velasco. It was dry but cloudy. I reached Columbia on the Brazos River at four that afternoon.
Columbia is a small place, notable chiefly as the railway terminus [[the initials are uncertain]]. The railway crosses low, flat, fertile country, mostly prairie and sparsely settled, though a few wealthy planters live along it. The Brazos is the prettiest river I have seen in this part of the country. It is about a hundred yards wide and very deep, with cottonwoods, willows, and quicksand along its banks.
On Thursday morning I left Columbia aboard the steamboat Era No. 3 for Velasco, about fifty miles downriver. The sky remained cloudy, with wind from the east [[part of the description is lost]]. Eight miles below Columbia lies Brazoria, a small town in the riverside woods and the only town below Columbia. The boat stopped along the way to collect men of Bates’s regiment who had been hunting in the river bottoms and cutting poles for cookhouses at the river’s mouth. [[A further line is clipped.]]

<a id="s09p116"></a>

## 09-116 — Delivering a prisoner and finding Dock at Velasco

Four or five miles above Velasco, the woods end and the river emerges onto open prairie. Like Sabine, the land is low and flat, with riverbanks scarcely eighteen inches high. Near the edge of the woods stood some of what I call Magruder’s “ground hoggery”: a small field fort with two openings for guns. Another earthwork, holding one gun, stands about two miles above the mouth.
We arrived at Velasco around three o’clock, with a strong, cold east wind. I found headquarters [[the following abbreviation is uncertain]] and delivered my prisoner as ordered. Then I looked for Dock. I soon found him but would not have recognized him. His beard has not seen a razor for years, and he is about as gray as Father. He was healthy but very tired of the war and eager for news from home: he had heard none since the battle of Shiloh.
Velasco lies east of the river and Quintana west. Most troops and headquarters are at Quintana. Velasco has only five or six houses, and Quintana about twice as many. The two “cities” face each other across about three hundred yards of river. There is no bay at the mouth. A fort stands on each side, right on the beach, covering the channel.

<a id="s09p117"></a>

## 09-117 — A schooner founders off Velasco

During the night there was considerable rain and a strong northeast wind. Two schooners were loaded to sail. One started out and struck the steamboat with her bow, damaging the [[bow?]] and a chimney. The schooner broke the main chain between her cutwater and bowsprit, but the crew did not notice until she reached the sea.
In deep water and heavy swells, she sprang a leak and had to return. The crew worked the pumps, but water entered so fast that she sank less than half a mile from shore, inside the outer bar on the sheltered side of the channel. The sea was too rough for the crew to leave before daylight, even though water washed over the decks.
At dawn, three men set out in the schooner’s only small boat. Four remained [[in the rigging?]], none able to swim. The boat soon capsized in the breakers. [[One?]] man let go and swam ashore; the other [[two?]] held onto the boat and drifted ashore four miles [[down?]] the beach.
The waves were so high that no boat could leave shore to help the men on the schooner, though they were less than half a mile away. Only a lifeboat could have survived in such a sea.

## 09-118 — Men stranded in the rigging; George returns to Houston

The nearest lifeboat was at the wreck of the Acacia, ten miles upwind along the beach. No boat could be launched into the open sea in such a gale. When our boat left, nothing had been done for the men clinging to the ropes. Without an available lifeboat, nothing could be done. On Friday I returned to Columbia in rain and unpleasant cold. Rain continued through Friday night and Saturday. I reached Houston on Saturday night and found deep, thin mud. Wharton’s death had caused an uproar, but everything was quiet again.

## 09-121 — George returns to Galveston, May 4, 1865

Galveston, May 4, 1865. Sister Lizzie,
We have moved here since I last wrote. Yesterday we left our old quarters after breakfast, traveled by special train, and settled into the new quarters in time for supper. In fine weather, it was our most pleasant move yet.
On May 2, Colonel Spaight reviewed us, and we marched through central Houston in platoon columns. ## 09-123 — A damaged and deserted Galveston, May 7, 1865

Galveston, Texas, May 7, 1865. Sister Lizzie,
Your letter of April 22 arrived last evening. One letter must be missing, since the correspondence does not quite connect. I think I forgot what I promised to tell you after returning from Velasco. It was only that Jody might go home; at that point it was still a prospect.
The captain says to tell Jody not to hurry back and to get what he went for, if possible. No one has come in, so he cannot give an extension of time. Jody will not be expected before June 1, and not especially then unless transportation is convenient.
Galveston has suffered badly. Signs of rough treatment are everywhere. It is much less attractive than two years ago, though still prettier than Houston. Business has stopped and the city looks deserted. Most wealthier residents have left. Poor people remain without employment; I cannot tell how they survive. I have seen no money circulating. Business is in Houston, while Galveston is neglected and forgotten.
Several regiments are quartered all over the city. Wherever they have stayed, they have ruined the houses. The Tremont House and Island City Hotel are worthless. Fences and old houses have been burned for fuel wherever an excuse could be found. Magruder has filled this end of the island with ditches and holes, ruining the landscape. An uninterrupted breastwork runs from the South Battery to Fort [[Scurry?]] and farther than I know. Lieutenant Colonel Bankhead ordered the sandhills between the battery and fort leveled, but the wind is rapidly rebuilding them. ## 09-124 — Rumors that the war is over—and George’s explicit caution

Now for the news. Rumors are numerous and varied. At least this seems clear: Johnston has surrendered, or is so close that he cannot avoid it. You may know as much of the details as I do.
Behind the scenes, there have been rumors of unpublished news. Flake of the Bulletin admitted as much. I have heard a little: Colonel Wilkes has arrived on parole from New Orleans. According to him, Sherman and Johnston agreed that Johnston would surrender Confederate forces everywhere, place their weapons in state arsenals, disband the troops, and send them home. State governments, laws, and officers would remain, but renounce the Confederacy, renew allegiance to the United States, and return to the Union. He says a forty-day armistice was arranged to consult the Washington cabinet, which rejected the proposal because it recognized state governments. He also reports that Grant would support Sherman with his army, treating the agreement as a military surrender approved by Breckenridge and Davis. That is the story, if my information is correct.
Warren told me this, and he is not one to jump to such a conclusion. He believes the war is over and the news is being withheld until official orders tell this department to give up and go home, allowing the change to happen quietly. If this is not premature, a couple of months will finish the drama. Much may be getting ahead of the facts, but the mystery around the published accounts encourages speculation.
Wilkes also brings unpublished news that Stoneman raided Johnson’s rear and destroyed immense quantities of supplies, railroads, and bridges. Take all this for what it is worth: I cannot vouch [[for its correctness; the last line is partly lost]].

## 09-125 — February 26, 1865: sore eyes, spring weather, and Confederate money

Houston, February 26, 1865. Sister Lizzie,
Your letter of the 11th arrived a day or two ago. I am glad your sore eyes have recovered. My own limited experience shows how unpleasant they are, though fortunately they have troubled me little so far.
The past week brought both fair and foul weather, with considerable rain overall. Friday and Friday night were very wet, but Saturday was fine and the mud is drying quickly today. I think spring has begun here. There may be a few cold days yet, but I expect none severe enough to cause harm.
Prices are slightly higher than last week. Confederate money has risen to “25” [[the exchange basis is unstated]], with little circulating. There seems to be an effort to reduce its circulation. Restoring its value would be an extraordinary achievement. I think only uncompromising [[tyranny?]] under military supervision could make it good again—and we will get plenty of that in the coming year, in every form to suit those providing it.

<a id="s09p126"></a>

## 09-126 — George mocks appeals to “military necessity” and unlimited sacrifice

There is little war news this week. Since peace prospects collapsed, no important fighting has been reported. Very little truth gets out nowadays, probably because Confederate affairs east of the Mississippi are so precarious.
We supposedly have freedom of conscience, speech, and the press under military [[despotism?]]. They are certainly “under” it—buried so deeply that they may never get out. So be it! It is amusing to watch states’ rights and every other defense of liberty patched up and excused as “military necessity.” Anyone who complains about government action is called unpatriotic whenever its final purpose is said to be worthy.
By that reasoning, any means become proper, just, and even praiseworthy if they accomplish the smallest good. A “good patriot” must surrender property, produce, and personal freedom to the government “for the time being” to achieve the “glorious end” of Southern independence. The last [[woman?]] and child must be sacrificed on liberty’s altar rather than accept Yankee rule; annihilation is supposedly preferable to such “slavery.” Anyone who will not give everything is called a coward and a scoundrel. That is the spirit of today’s fire-eating press.

## 09-127 — The loudest war advocates—and an unkind assessment of city fashions

Brave editors behind desks, brave legislators in Congress, brave old men smoking by the fireplace, and brave boys too young for conscription can talk about what “we’ll do” to the Yankees and how long “we’ll fight.” Men who have never smelled gunpowder and never will are ready to fight longer than anyone else. Those who talk most about fighting do none of it. Some patriotic firebrands who promised to spill the “last drop of blood” unknowingly spoke the truth: they will be the last to spill any blood.
I went to the Methodist Episcopal church today. It was full of soldiers, civilians, and women. To me, this city seems to have more unattractive women than anywhere I have been. I cannot remember seeing a handsome one here. They spoil their appearance with excessive clothing and fashionable touches that suggest a [[soft?]] spot in the brain. Their love of novelty—or vanity—and desire to display something new and attract attention do not suit me. It reminds me of Burns: if some power gave us the gift of seeing ourselves as others see us, it would spare us many a blunder and foolish notion.

## 09-128 — Children playing bring George’s childhood to mind

6 p.m. This evening I walked through the western part of the city. Compared with the business district, it made an interesting picture. Everything was quiet, with people in their Sunday clothes. Some walked in the mild spring air and sunshine, but most sat together in their attractive parlors, apparently enjoying themselves.
Comfortable cottages and their associations with home are pleasant to see, even if I cannot enjoy them myself now. Merry children playing on sunny slopes and grassy lawns reminded me of fondly remembered days. Those scenes cannot return, but similar sights keep them fresh in memory. Nothing recalls the past as quickly as a group of happy children.
The church bell is ringing, and I intend to go. I will finish and mail this so it can leave tomorrow. I do not remember Miss Luna or Kate [[Denny?]], but I do remember Miss Lizzie K. Tell the girls to enjoy themselves while they are young; when they are old, perhaps they cannot.
With love to all, your brother, George.

## 09-129 — Houston, December 11, 1864: yellow fever and church music

Houston, December 11, 1864. Sister Lizzie,
Your letter of November 19 has arrived. It found everyone well in body and mind, as far as I can judge. We have had plenty of cool and some cold weather this week. I think it is enough to clear the city of yellow fever unless the weather changes again [[part of the phrase is obscured]].
There has been much fever lately, confined to civilians’ houses. Among people returning since the November 20 freeze, cases have occurred only in houses where it had been during summer. I am not afraid of an outbreak among the troops, since we occupy houses without cases this season.
This morning I attended the Methodist church. Neither preacher nor sermon was exceptional, and the congregation was small for a city. An organ stood in one corner. A large Black man worked its pumping handle while a woman skillfully played the keys.

Reading note: The account preserves George’s understanding of yellow fever at the time; it is not a modern explanation of transmission.

## 09-130 — George compares Methodist and Episcopal worship

To me, instrumental music is as out of place in church as a dog in a parlor. I felt I was at a sermon until the organ began; then it seemed like a party. I cannot find support in the New Testament for music in churches—unless it hides in some corner I have overlooked.
Last Sunday I attended the Episcopal church. It is a large, magnificent brick building in Gothic style, which I associate with Catholic churches. The minister emerged from a side room in a robe, knelt for private prayer where everyone could see him, and began the service. Minister and congregation alternated reading verses aloud, and the congregation repeated his prayer sentences. They sang psalms instead of hymns. These sounded very good, with some variation in tune but perfect harmony.
Afterward the younger members were questioned on the catechism to assess their progress toward full membership, including reciting the creed. I consider the creed identical to the Presbyterians’ [[the next words are obscured]], and very good if they practice what they preach.
Infants are baptized with two sponsors who promise to raise them, or ensure they are properly and religiously raised, if their parents die or cannot care for them. There is much ceremony, resembling Catholic worship. The preaching is fairly plain, but I dislike the fashion and display around it.
I largely agree with Fanny Fern: “True religion may go to Church in yellow gloves—”

## 09-131 — Wealth, religious display, and George’s preference for modest dress

“—and embroidered handkerchiefs worth five hundred dollars, but I don’t believe it!” Still, I do not know that I have any right to judge Houston’s people simply because they are wealthy.
I have noticed several new styles of women’s hats. I prefer high-crowned, narrow-brimmed leghorn hats to the broad-brimmed styles once so conspicuous on the prairies. Even so, in my opinion the old-fashioned close “split” bonnet is the prettiest thing a woman ever wore on her head.
You may call that outdated in an age of showy display, when brilliance is all the [[rage?]]. But it is modest, and I consider modesty a woman’s finest quality, whatever the world says. Above all, be modest.

## 09-132 — Auctions, flour supplies, and Sherman’s move into Georgia

Sunday, December 11, 7 p.m. I am writing by candlelight again, adding a few thoughts in my usual disconnected way. The boys are going to the Methodist church, leaving me alone. It is too cold for me to go unless they abandon the music and provide preaching instead of entertainment.
There are auctions almost daily, offering nearly every kind of dry goods at very high, though slightly falling, prices. Flour is plentiful wholesale and retail, in quantities to suit buyers. Asked where it comes from, people always say “up the country.” I have not heard of much being sold to civilians there; I thought the government took it all for the army.
The latest news is somewhat stirring. Sherman has made a bold move into Georgia, surprising everyone and causing excitement in military circles. For once, he has outmaneuvered Hood and all his advisers. [[The final words are clipped.]]

## 09-133 — Guarded soldiers’ opinions, private gossip, and expected emancipation

The report that Magruder was ordered back to Texas is wrong. It is strange how people who should know better start and believe such rumors. Walker will remain in command, and furloughs will continue as usual. Spaight is trying to recall everyone on detached duty. With all present, the regiment would number nearly a thousand, making it about the largest in the state.
About a dozen men from our company are home on leave. Only one has married so far: according to reports, the old bachelor Eddings married a widow in [[Stewart?]] a few days ago. He has not returned.
You were somewhat mistaken at “Shialot” about the war question. Soldiers generally profess more patriotism than they feel because they do not know whom they are dealing with and prefer to be safe.
As for the marriage matter, I was very surprised. Although I often act by fits and starts, this one stumped me. I did not know that she had done anything disreputable. I heard Henry had renounced his intentions, but thought it was a lovers’ matter. What else I heard only confirmed an old opinion about a lack of moral courage and resolve. I know no more than I knew three years ago about what has happened since I left home or entered service, and I do not care to know. Let the subject drop. [[He?]] had better forget marriage and go to Mexico to escape these troubled times.
Black people are bound to be freed before the war ends, and Jeff will not give up while he can get a white or Black man to fight for him. “Mr. Southern is something by himself.”

## 09-134 — Blockade running, conscription, and a cold room

A schooner has just reached the bayou landing from Tampico with merchandise. It belongs to [[Reichman?]] and Myers of this city.
Many people along the coast avoid service through blockade running, though they do not sail on the vessels themselves. A few men are gathering conscripts and sending them to the front. They should instead collect idle, useless officers and put them where they belong, rather than trouble honest men.
It is late and cold, and our room has no fire, so I will close. I must take this to the post office tonight so it can leave for Millican in the morning.
With love to all, your brother, George Douglas.
