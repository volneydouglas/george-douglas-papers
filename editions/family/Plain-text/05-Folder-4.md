## 07-003 — Lizzie fears for George’s safety and the missing letters

Brushy, Wednesday night, April 15, 1863. Letter No. 10.
Dear Brother,
I am writing again, although I cannot promise anything very interesting. Last Saturday Laura received your March 21 letter—the first we had received for three weeks. On Sunday Lou received Joda’s letter with your April 12 letter enclosed. We were very glad, because we did not know whether you had left Galveston.

I hope your new posting is healthy and pleasant, though I fear it will be less so than the one you left. Do you think you could be attacked so far inland? It seems a curious place for soldiers. I fear you will not stay there long, but will keep being sent onward until you reach the Mississippi, where you might be trapped like the prisoners at the Post, or suffer a worse fate.

## 07-004 — Mother’s fears, Lizzie’s request to burn letters, and farm work

—my letters to you, and perhaps something dreadful had happened to you. You might have carelessly left them lying around. Who knows how much treason someone might find in our unpopular ideas? That was Mother’s reasoning; only time could show whether she was right. I know you will smile at this, but remember how easily frightened we become when anything unusual happens.

Mother has also warned me to be careful what I write in these dangerous times. While I remember, Beck told us she found several of my old letters in Warren’s trunk. After you have read my letters as much as you like, would it not be better to burn them in your campfire? They will make the fire burn a little higher, then settle down and cause no trouble.

After hoeing hard all day, carrying a bucket of water from the main spring and milking the cows—we have six young calves in the pen—I feel very tired. Hoping you are not on guard tonight, I will say goodnight and retire.

April 16. It is a beautiful evening. A strong southern breeze has blown away the clouds that hid the sun this morning.

Closing written in the upper margin:
Everyone here on Brushy is well. No more for now. Affectionately, Lizzie.

Reading note: “Treason” is the family’s feared interpretation of their private letters. The passage does not specify a charge, prosecution or particular political position. It also directly records Lizzie’s hoeing, water carrying and milking.

## 07-008 — Lizzie reports Mrs. Payne’s death and worries over the mail

Saturday night, April 4 [year not stated; possibly 1863].
Dear George,
Warren’s visit is nearly over, since he leaves tomorrow. I thought I would write a few lines to tell you that we are all up and about. We have had no letter from you for about two weeks. I cannot understand why, unless you have been stopped from writing; I hope that is not the case.

This evening we were saddened to hear that Mrs. Payne has died. Poor woman, leaving her little children to face an unkind world without a mother’s loving care. There are five children, including a one-week-old baby. Preston is home now, though of course he will soon have to return to the army.

Reading note: The letter reports the death and children but does not give Mrs. Payne’s first name. Preston’s relationship to her is not explicitly stated in this passage.

## 07-009 — Cotton socks, medicine, and longing for the family to reunite

We are sending you two pairs of socks, some pills and a fine comb. I wish we could send something else, but I have thought about it and cannot think of anything else that would help you. You seemed to like the cotton socks Ellen gave you, so we knitted more, thinking they would be more comfortable in summer. If you do not like them, I suppose you have enough wool socks until we can send more.

Sister is visiting, and Warren is here. With you and Joda here too, things would feel as they used to. I hope the time comes when you can be with us again, but if you have gone east, I fear it will be a long wait. I sent you a long letter in yesterday’s mail, so I have no more interesting news tonight.
Your sister, Lizzie.

## 07-016 — Typed copy: criticism of military authority and a plea for peace

—I will try to make my letters more interesting.

You ask what I think of the opinions your commander expresses. To say the least, they are arbitrary, unjust and tyrannical. If people like that gain power, what happens to our liberty and our rights as thinking people? Gone—gone forever!

I am sorry you serve under someone with so little feeling and humanity, so intent on imposing his opinions. If he believes what he says, that is bad enough; if he does not, I despise his principles and consider him unfit for his position. If he saw this letter, he would probably be more convinced than ever that correspondence should stop between the “utensils” of war and their supposedly worthless friends at home. I hope he never receives a higher command, but is reduced to an ordinary private. Then let him tyrannize over anyone he can.

We cannot hope for peace while the North continues its campaign and the South its stubborn resistance. If only those in power would stop and think what this struggle is for, and why the blood keeps flowing. But they do not. The love of power is strongest, and everything must serve it. We cannot stop it; we can only be carried along by this great current and trust Providence to bring us safely through. I pray that peace returns soon.

It is Saturday night, my usual time to write to you. Sue, Laura and I are writing; Mother is knitting; Father and the children are in bed.

Reading note: This forceful political passage is read from a later typed copy in the scan. The commander is unnamed; the text alone cannot identify him or establish the exact policy that prompted the criticism.

## 07-017 — Typed copy: sickness at home and uncertainty after a lighthouse fight

Brushy, May 7, 1863.
Dear George,
Laura is sick, so it falls to me to write again this week. We were getting ready for church last Sunday when her stomach became upset. She was quite ill until this morning. Now she can sit up a little and hopes to write this evening. Everyone else is well except me: I woke dizzy this morning. [The typed copy has an unreadable or omitted passage marked with question marks, ending with “further sickness.”]

Your letter covering the 8th through the 15th arrived the day I posted my No. 12. Laura received your letter of the 16th and 17th that same day. Hers was written just before the lighthouse fight reported in the Telegraph, so we were anxious for your next letter to learn whether you and Joda took part. If so, I hope you were unharmed; the paper reported no wounded on our side.

We very much hope you have not been sent to Louisiana. Troops will presumably have to remain at Sabine, and since you are there, perhaps you will stay. But orders seem to send men one way today and somewhere entirely different tomorrow. Stone’s regiment had traveled toward the frontier for three or four days when the order was reversed and they were sent to Louisiana. We heard that about four hundred refused and a serious disturbance nearly followed. I cannot say how true that report is. From what I hear, that regiment seems unreliable, and I doubt the Confederacy benefits much from its service.

## 07-028 — Lizzie’s reply to the opinions of George’s commander

Saturday, February 14.
Your February 6 letter arrived this morning. I am glad you are healthy. I see that I must lengthen my letters—spin a longer thread, or try to compose a little. I cannot hope to become a newspaper correspondent. My mind is not full enough of ideas to communicate to the public, even if I had the words to do it. ## 07-031 — Laura is ill; the family waits for news of the lighthouse fight

Brushy, May 7, 1863.
Dear George,
Laura is ill, so it is my turn to write again this week. We were preparing to go to church last Sunday when she developed a sick stomach. She has been quite ill since then, but this morning she can sit up a little and thinks she may write this evening. Everyone else is well except me. I woke feeling dizzy. [[A line about her health is lost at a fold.]]

I received your letter of the 8th through the 15th on the day I mailed my No. 12. Laura received yours of the 16th and 17th that day too. Hers was written just before the lighthouse fight reported in the Telegraph, so we are anxious for your next letter to learn whether you and Joda took part. If you did, I hope you are unhurt. The newspaper mentioned no wounded on our side.

We hope very much you have not been sent to Louisiana. Troops will presumably have to stay at Sabine, and perhaps you will remain there. It is uncertain: soldiers are ordered in one direction today and sent somewhere entirely different tomorrow. Stone’s regiment marched toward the frontier for three or four days before its orders were reversed and it was sent to Louisiana. ## 07-065 — George to his sisters: cold weather and desertion, January 11, 1864

Another unpleasant week has passed. Sleet fell, the ground froze fairly hard, and the wind has been damp and chilly. Today is cloudy, rain threatens, and mud is everywhere. The claim that this latitude is warm in winter is a joke. It is less cold than Ellis County, but the damp makes it more uncomfortable than dry cold. All things considered, we are doing well for soldiers and should not complain. Living in houses is much better than sleeping outdoors without tents, as the men around Lavaca must. We have enough beef and bread to live reasonably well—[[illegible wording]]—much better than some others.

The latest news from the east is discouraging. The Yankees are jubilant and the Confederates downcast. Everything looks dark for the Confederacy. Our armies are in bad condition, while the enemy’s are the opposite. Our men all want to go home; the Yankees say they will reenlist. See the Telegraph of January 8. The militia are very homesick and intend to go home when their six months end, whatever happens. Fifty men have deserted our battalion since the big scouting expedition in December. Some are reportedly captured, but I doubt it: the report came indirectly. Eight or ten Davis Guards have also deserted to the Yankees. The company resents harsh treatment. One man was put in a ball and chain for a minor offense. They say Magruder had promised never to punish them in such a degrading way. They petitioned him to cancel the sentence, and he [[illegible: final line faded]].

Since the first case, another one or three men have been put in a ball and chain. They threaten to surrender the fort to the Yankees next time unless they receive better treatment. We will see how much truth there is in this. If the Irish soldiers carry out their threat, it will be serious.

Griffin told the battalion that resistance to the court-martial’s decree was mutiny. ## 07-067 — George criticizes discipline and doubts the Confederacy’s future

—and the men originally involved [[unclear opening wording]], the others quietly gave in and allowed them to wear the barrels. Company C showed more willingness to mutiny than the others. ## 07-068 — Impressment, defeat, and a faint hope of peace

## 07-075 — Food, gardens, and artillery drill, July 10, 1864

Manhassett, July 10, 1864. No. [[77?]]. Dear sister,
Your June 26 letter arrived last Friday and found us all well. Things are dull, though we had a good rain recently. We manage to eat fairly well for these times: today we had fresh corn and tomato soup. That is good fare for a Confederate soldier, especially two or three times a week. We could eat much better if someone brought in garden seeds from abroad. Vegetables deteriorate so quickly here that their stock runs out within two or three years. I am told beets do not produce seed here and cabbages form heads only in the first year. [[The next sentence about locally grown vegetables is unclear.]] Melons and grapes do well, and I have seen fairly good corn, cotton, and tobacco. Sweet and Irish potatoes grow well, but Irish potatoes have nearly disappeared because people depended on northern seed supplies and the blockade cut them off. If I owned land here, I would plant a vineyard. The soil seems suited to all kinds of grapes. Mustang grapes grow wild, and “French” grapes do fairly well in gardens. This is a fine country except for the mosquitoes.

We have done little around camp for three or four weeks, but this week we will have more lessons on the big guns. Artillery drill is easier to learn than infantry drill, but I dislike being around the guns in battle: they attract shot, bombs, and shrapnel. Shrapnel is a spherical shell filled with musket balls, with one thick side and one thin side. The thick side goes toward the powder; the thin side faces forward. When it bursts, the balls fly forward like a blunderbuss and can kill or disable everyone serving a gun. [[The final phrase, referring to our cannon and Calcasieu, is illegible.]]

## 07-091 — Rebecca praises George’s principles, May 12, 1863

At home, May 12, 1863. Brother George,
Yesterday I received your kind letter of April 20. I was glad to hear from you; I feared my own letter had not reached you. I am proud of your principles, George. Noble-minded people will respect you for them. When the clouds of war and the dark passions poisoning the air have passed, those principles will be a halo of glory around your head—or, better still, peace around your heart. They will never make you sigh with regret or blush with shame. If you die, they will be our only comfort. If you live, they will assure us that the hopes now gathering around you—

<a id="s07p92"></a>

## 07-092 — Rebecca’s hopes for George and news of health at home

—will not end in bitter disappointment. I confess I feared for you: you were young, and so much advice and example pulled you the other way. If you overcome that pressure now, you will earn people’s trust for the future. My deepest wish is that you pass through this ordeal with your mind unscarred and your body unharmed.

We are all reasonably well, and there is little illness nearby that I know of. Little Lucy is stronger and has started growing. Sister Laura was quite ill a week or more ago, but she was improving when I last heard. Early spring was far too wet; lately the weather has been suitable. The weeds seem to grow much faster than usual—

## 07-093 — Buying steers and arranging repayment

We need an army at home to fight the weeds. They are formidable, and every available worker has been pressed into service.

May [[15?]]. I delayed finishing until I had news of Collins’s beef steer. Today I heard that it is on his range and fat. ## 07-094 — Marriages, harvest, and concern for prisoners

Old Johnny Chapman has married the widow Hogue; Jo Porter, the widow Strong; and George Clayton, Lavinia Andrews. Gurley’s regiment has been dismounted and ordered east. Nearly all Stone’s men are home preparing for the harvest. Wheat will ripen next week and looks better than usual; the corn is behind. We hear little reliable war news. ## 07-097 — Rebecca visits the Mullican neighborhood, September 4, 1864

Sunday, September 4, 1864. Brother George,
Your letter of August 14 arrived a few days ago. Your ideas about how long the war will last and what may affect it seem reasonable from your position. I will not debate them, but instead describe my visit to the Mullicans’ neighborhood two weeks ago.
It was my second visit since they moved, and I took all the children. They were all well. Martha and Matilda are almost grown: attractive, modest girls. Henry and John had grown so much I hardly knew them. George and Orlando have died. Thomas and Monroe are in military service. We saw Thomas’s wife. [[Coz?]] Mullican looks well, but Mrs. Mullican looks unwell. Mrs. Vaughn, Josh Vaughn, Ruth, and the children look very unwell.

<a id="s07p98"></a>

## 07-098 — A preacher interprets Revelation in wartime

William’s children look well. Brother Robinson preached at the Mullicans’ house on Saturday night and at the schoolhouse on Sunday. I saw Jimmy Myers and his wife. Jimmy looks the same; his wife is a large, attractive woman. On the way down we met a man with one arm, and another one-armed man stayed at the Mullicans’ that night. The preacher was enthusiastic and seemed to know the Bible well.
During a conversation about Revelation, he argued that the two witnesses in chapter 11 represent the Old and New Testaments. He believed their prophesying began with the rise of the Roman Catholic Church, and that its 1,260-year period ended when this war began.

<a id="s07p99"></a>

## 07-099 — Robinson’s wartime prophecy, continued

He said the three and a half years during which their bodies would lie in the street are almost over. Then God’s spirit will enter them and they will be taken into heaven—meaning, he believed, that the Testaments will regain their proper place in the church and in Christians’ minds.
He interpreted the earthquake as the fall of a government, and the fall of a tenth of the city as the loss of the Church of Rome’s power. He connected this with conscription, claiming it interfered with the Pope’s authority because the Pope opposed Catholics’ participation in the war. He predicted that the Pope’s political power would end in 1866. A period of changes would follow, the most important of which—

<a id="s07p100"></a>

## 07-100 — Rebecca’s hope for peace through prophecy

—would be the spread of what he called true Christianity. Then the seventh angel would sound, and voices in heaven would proclaim that the kingdoms of the world had become the kingdoms of the Lord and his Christ, who would reign forever.
Brother Robinson explained it much better than I can write it, making everything fit neatly. I cannot remember enjoying anything so much. Imagine it: the Bible restored, the war ended, and Roman Catholicism brought down, along with the gradual disappearance of error and Christianity’s final triumph! May it be so. Rebecca.

## 07-102 — Rebecca considers moving to the Nueces, December 11, 1864

Sunday, December 11, 1864. Brother George,
I imagine you are settled in camp again, and perhaps a letter from me will relieve some lonely hours. We are all reasonably well, though my own health is poor. I hope winter will bring improvement.
It has been cold since Wednesday, and people are busy slaughtering hogs. My own prospects for pork are slim, probably because the only person who could look after the hogs is away.
Lately I have been thinking of moving. If I can manage it, I will leave in the spring and go down to the Nueces—

<a id="s07p103"></a>

## 07-103 — Cotton hauling, cattle, and a proposed home near Fort Ewel

Norvell has written asking me to come and bring the cattle if I can. That would be a major undertaking now: the cattle are scattered everywhere, and workers will be hard to find.
He intends to haul forty bales in the spring. Since he has to be away from home, he might as well make it pay. He currently has four teams on the road—or perhaps already at Brownsville—hauling thirty bales from San Antonio for nine cents a pound.
He will settle into winter quarters around Christmas. I expect to live near Fort Ewel, where he can stop for buttermilk as he passes. He has hired a “Dutchman” and two boys, besides Rob and another man. He bought two wagons, twelve pairs of oxen, a horse, revolvers, and Mexican blankets for $800.

<a id="s07p104"></a>

## 07-104 — Rebecca asks whether the war can continue

I have not visited Father since Henry left, though I saw Laura the other day. Everyone was reasonably well. They expected Warren last night. I meant to visit today but was afraid to go out in the cold.
I do not know what to tell you about the girls, since you did not get to know my darlings. You must get to know them when you next come.
George, what about the war now? Will it ever end, and how? Will the army stay another four years—can it? After all the South’s boasts, what remains but surrender or destruction? How long before every man has a duty to think and act for himself? Those bigots willing to see our men killed, our country ruined, and our women and children starved should be quiet.

<a id="s07p105"></a>

## 07-105 — Fear of requisitions, drought, and a hoped-for adobe house

Price’s army has been driven back to Texas for the winter. I hear that for eighty miles south of the Red River they are taking horses two years old and older, mares, mules, oxen, and all the beef cattle. Some may come here for food. By next summer, I expect this area to feel all the horrors of war. Whether an enemy invades may hardly matter: unless peace comes, we will be stripped of everything. There is no use talking now; we are fighting.

It is still very dry. We have had enough rain to sprout the wheat, but not enough to fill the pools in the creek or replenish our little well. Write occasionally, and when we have built our adobe house, come and visit. Rebecca.
