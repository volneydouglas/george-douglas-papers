Orange County, Texas, June 28, 1864 [date faint]

Cousin George, dear sir,

Having a chance to send you a note, I take pleasure in telling you that I am in good health and fine spirits. Our regiment left Alexandria, Louisiana, a week ago yesterday, for [destination indistinct]. I was detailed to go down Red River and join the command at [Walker's Bend?], but it has not passed. Brushy, Ellis County, Texas
Friday, January [29], 1864

Mr. George Douglas—dear cousin,

The heading tells you where I am. After an absence of twenty-one months, I arrived here on the second or third of this month. Since then, I have mingled with the friends of my youth. Truly, I have had a pleasant time. The warm welcome and friendly kindness shown to me have been deeply gratifying.

I have just returned from Aunt Bettie's and thought I would chat with you for a while through this substitute for a visit.

I wrote to you last September but have never learned what became of my letter. Either you did not receive it, or you did not reply. I hope this one will not offend you.

Since last October, I have been on detached service most of the time. Our regiment was sent to the Ouachita River country to gather [men described in the letter as “Yaps”?] for the conscription authorities. I spent two months on this business. Part of my soldiering passed more pleasantly. The first month expired, and [a phrase about provisions is obscured]. As a matter of course, someone who pleases others must please the ladies. In brilliant [evening gatherings], smiles, and [laughter], and where the pot boiled most temptingly, I was not entirely unaware of the gentle sex's influence. On some occasions, I acknowledged their claims.

I admit I found the young ladies of my [district?] attractive—I like to give the devil his due—but [a few connecting words are obscured]. The friends of my acquaintance [unclear] seem to have special claims to superiority. Enough of that, as it is hardly suitable in these times of strife and confusion, when the absorbing theme is war, war: its stern realities and consequences. Our country is now beginning to feel its weight considerably.

[Opening of scan page 5, continuing onto scan page 7]

Now, something about our movements. In the latter part of the fall, a portion of our regiment was sent out to assist the Louisianans in getting men into the service. We all seem to have arrived at the same [time or place]. Colonel Burleson, having become famous as a “Rosinheel” catcher, was sent into this state on the same business. We were mortified to learn that Texas contained quite a number of people of that description.

I joined the command in Rusk County at the end of November. Soon afterward, I volunteered to take some prisoners to Tyler and was then detailed to deliver them at Shreveport. There I was ordered to take some [men or items; unclear] to Bonham. This suited me rather well. I thought the Shreveport commander might object to us visiting home, so we decided to go on to Bonham.

Our company was allowed to spend the holidays in this county. I stayed at home about fifteen days, then reported at Corsicana, and from there was sent to San Augustine. I would prefer not to have anything to do with this business; it is by no means agreeable to me.

Our brigade is ordered to be at Hempstead, Texas, by February 10. It is moving by way of Shreveport. I hope our detachment will be allowed to join it on the march. I leave Monday for the command. At first, I received a twenty-day furlough. Before it expired, I was detailed to transact some business here.

Clothing is being issued to the company every [month?]. I have not drawn a blanket yet. [Irvin?] Lavender, of Ross's regiment, is home on furlough. His squad brought some arms across the [river; word unclear]. [A reference to men under “Copp… Boudy” is obscured.] He reports that the Mississippi army is well supplied and in high spirits.

We have not heard from [Yapett?] and James since the middle of October. [Name unclear] was doing well after the wounds he received in the Chickamauga battle. I am very anxious to know how they came out of the Chattanooga fight.

Captain Bledsoe left Lancaster a few days ago to resume command of his company. That will be gratifying to them, as they were not pleased with the captain placed over them when their command was consolidated.

The last company of the militia has been called out, 200 and more, en route for Galveston. All [460?] were mustered into the service. Those unable to go now have received furloughs lasting from one to six months.

## Pages 10-11
### Henry offers sympathy and reports from camp

Camp [Waverly?], Texas
March 13, 1865

Cousin George, dear sir,

I acknowledge your kind letter of the twelfth. It stirred many pleasant feelings, especially the part about our association in years gone by. While enjoying these times over again in imagination and reflecting on the return of a long-absent mother, I was saddened by news of your sister's death.

All look forward with mingled feelings of gladness and sorrow to the end of trying scenes such as these. In this dark hour of bereavement, you have my sympathy. No longer can you look to her cheerful, homelike presence. [A short connecting phrase is unclear.] The fluttering of hope is overtaken by the cold reality. By it, we are warned that we are mortal. Let us use these finer feelings of our nature for our comfort and in the performance of our duty.

[Scan page 11]

We are all well here and continue to have agreeable times. We came to this place on March 8 and will very likely go to the Brazos country. I suppose the forage in this section will be exhausted in ten days or two weeks.

[George B.'s?] arrival was very unexpected to me. I had greatly feared he had not survived the Tennessee campaign. Through him, I heard a letter from Brother James, which I enclose to you.

Lafayette wants to transfer to this department. Mr. Browne of this company wants to transfer to Lafayette's command and has written to Captain [Ward?] on the subject. The captain will make an effort to arrange it. Nothing could give me more heartfelt pleasure.

I am looking for [Tayett?] and Lumber [Rea?] within a few days. We recently heard from Aunt Bettie. She was quite sick.

Address me at Hempstead, which is [our post-office address?].

Much love to you, and goodbye.

Your cousin,
Henry

## Pages 12-14
### “The River of Death,” copied for Lizzie

[Scan page 14 labels this a ballad, “The River of Death,” written for Lizzie. The accompanying note says it was copied, so this does not establish that Henry composed it. The poem below preserves all seven stanzas in modernized language.]

[Scan page 12]

Beautiful world! I trusted you for so long—
Your glittering show, your siren song.
I thought the joy you gave would last,
But one by one, those joys rushed past,
Until the last you could give has flown,
And I stand by the river, alone! Alone!

I was merry once; to music sweet,
I kept time with my dancing feet.
I crowned my hair with roses red,
But the lute is broken, the roses dead.
Music and flowers have long since gone,
And I stand by the river, alone! Alone!

And once I loved—it was a dream of bliss.
Could earth give her child more than this?
Such rapture that it was almost pain,
A garland of flowers, a silken chain.
But love, like a vision at night, has flown,
And I stand by the river, alone! Alone!

[Scan page 13]

Then madly I drank from the cup of fame,
And smiled when thousands praised my name.
My song was heard with wonder and awe,
But quickly it faded and drifted away.
Leaf after leaf, until all were gone,
And I stand by the river, alone! Alone!

And what have I done, O Christ, for you?
Draw back the veil from my dim eyes!
Have I given a cup of cold water
To one little lamb in your precious fold?
The time for work is past and gone,
And I stand by the river, alone! Alone!

O Christ, have mercy! The wild waters
Are sweeping away your wandering child.
Will you not listen? Oh, take my hand,
And guide me through to the distant land.
All that I trusted on earth has flown.
Must I sink in the river, alone! Alone!

The river! The river! It is dark and wide,
But I see a light on the other side.
My heart no longer is faint with fear,
For sorrow has passed; my Lord is near.
With joyful steps I hurry on,
No more in the river, alone! Alone!

[Accompanying note on scan page 13]

Blossomdale, July 6, [1875?]

Cousin Lizzie,

You thought the few lines above, which I repeated on the night of the fourth, were beautiful. To indulge my inclination to scribble, I have written out a copy for you, trusting that you will receive it gladly. I think the lines are very beautiful. They are doubtless the work of some famous [writer; final word unclear].

Your cousin,
Henry

To Lizzie.

## Pages 17-18
### Henry writes from home about court, a camp meeting, and the war

[Willow Branch?], Texas
September [18], 1864

Cousin George,

I have written several times but have received only silence. Perhaps my uncertain whereabouts are the cause, for I cannot think you so ungenerous as not to have written since I left last January. Sometimes I have thought my letter of last spring contained something unacceptable. If so, I cannot imagine what it was, for if you are like most soldiers, your feelings could hardly be ruffled.

I will give you another shot. If silence is again the response, I will know something is wrong in Denmark and call off my hounds. As I am floating in a rather dull atmosphere, I cannot promise to be very interesting. Still, I hope to relieve your camp's monotony for a while.

I am home on detail to get clothing for our company. I arrived last Saturday evening. Things in this neighborhood are much as they were before the war, except for the absence of the young men. Plenty seems to smile at every door. Were it not for the absent ones, I think people here could scarcely believe a great war was wreaking havoc on their countrymen.

I have visited very little yet. I have been to Waxahachie twice. An important case is before the district court: the murder of Mrs. Clement by her daughter. [This is the writer's description of the charge, not an independently established finding.] The case has not yet been decided, but from the evidence I have heard, I believe she will be acquitted, though not considered innocent by the public.

On Monday, I went from Waxahachie to the Red Oak campground, where a camp meeting was underway, and stayed until Tuesday. Attendance was smaller than in former years, especially among the sterner sex, but there was no lack on the other side. The soldiers present received marked attention. I enjoyed those provisions that keep soul and body together, but I did not work hard to make myself agreeable or to spread what is sometimes called a fluttering of the heart. My observations suggest that the germs of that malady lie dormant and only need sunshine to grow strong and catching.

There were shouting times that night, though some things went the other way. Some sorrowful mourners were there, including a soldier friend who yielded to the urging of an old gentleman who had long pressed his claims upon him.

If shouting measures goodness, I think this meeting's standing is rather poor. It certainly contains the most impudent [or “profane”; word unclear] collection I have met. Perhaps this is partly due to the abundance of preachers, who claim exemption because of their calling and fear that unless they all preach, they will have to shoulder their muskets and fight the enemy. [These are the writer's opinions.]

[Scan page 18]

I will now tell you something of our command's operations. In June it was [in the west of Louisiana; the precise place is obscured]. A week or so later, [a name or unit is illegible] returned to the valley of Red River. We remained in that state for a month, mostly on [bayous?]. About August 1, we moved to the Mississippi Valley near Natchez. We stayed there a couple of weeks and were then ordered to our former field, Arkansas.

On August 31, the brigade started upriver. We had “seen the elephant” in Louisiana [experienced combat], and the boys were willing to go anywhere. They will doubtless see some other large animal in Arkansas, for they will have to find it if they expect [several crossed-out or obscured words]. I hope the boys will all get home by [the time is unclear]. They will have plenty of work with Steele. Major's men have also gone [destination or assignment unclear]. Walker's and Polignac's [forces are mentioned; connecting words obscured]. If so, you may live to hear of stirring times this fall.

While scouting along the Mississippi, our squadron turned over about thirty fine horses, with their equipment, to the Yankees. About half the men went down to New Orleans to get some crackers. The others escaped by running and hiding in the weeds. They were completely surprised at about three in the morning on August 28. Several were wounded and captured, but none were killed. Captain Veal was away from his command. He and the dismounted men are now at home getting new mounts. I was not in the scrape.

Jef and some of my mess, who lost their horses, had just come out on leave. Rob got to camp the day before I left. I had chills for about a month during the summer, was sick last evening, and expect them to return. Jeptha was down last week.

[Names unclear] and Lizzie [went or joined us] Sunday, with [names unclear]. Emma spent last night here. Brother has had a very sick girl; she is getting [better?]. We had a fine rain the day before yesterday. Parson Austin called in today on his way back from the meeting. It is still going on. Brother [McCamp?] has been indisposed, with only seven professions [of faith] so far.

We recently received a letter from Lafayette, dated the second of this month. Their brigade had a heavy fight on July 22. Their regiment entered the battle with 138 men and came out with about 80 [figures somewhat unclear]. James was wounded through the wrist and hand and was in hospital. Those of their company not killed had to surrender, while they were attacking [the] camps. Fayette threw down his [gun?] and ran out. He was still in good health.

My sheet is full. I hope to hear from you before I leave for the army.

Yours truly,
H. Douglas

## Pages 19-20
### Bob remembers hunting with George

Cold Springs, Texas
February 19, 1863

Mr. George Douglas—dear cousin,

You may think it strange to receive a letter from me, as you have probably forgotten your old hunting companion and schoolmate. It has been a long time since we used to meet down on old Brushy and take a shot at the old gobblers. I fear it will be longer still before we enjoy that blessed privilege again.

Nearly four years have passed since I saw you. I imagine you are the same old George you used to be. I have not changed, except from big to bigger. I weigh 175 pounds and am the biggest devil in the company.

This morning, I was ordered to report to brigade headquarters for fatigue duty. I went halfway, turned around, and came back. Now I am watching for the guard to arrest me. If they do, I will lie my way out somehow.

We are having a very pleasant time at this camp. Duty is easy: we drill an hour every day except Sunday, as before. We also have a chain of guards around the camp who will not let a fellow out with his saddle. You ought to see the boys riding off across the country bareback.

There is a big religious meeting in camp. About forty mourners were there last night. They have not got me yet, but I do not know how soon they will, for every time the old fellow preaches, I feel a lump in my throat as big as your fist.

[Scan page 20]

I have not heard from my brothers east of the Mississippi for two or three months. I fear they are having a hard time.

I will bring this badly composed letter to a close. Give my respects to all my friends.

As ever, your cousin,
[initials unclear]

P.S. Write and give me all the news. Tell me how you like the war and when you think it will end.

Bob

## Pages 21-24
### James describes his wounded hand and hopes of coming home

Tupelo, Mississippi
January 25, 1865

Dear brother,

Brother Lafayette will leave for home on furlough in a few days, so I will write a few lines to let you know how I am getting along.

I was painfully wounded on July 22 while charging the enemy's works. The ball struck my left wrist and passed through my hand. The wrist bones were badly fractured, as was the bone of my middle finger. I have suffered a great deal. Around the middle of August, I caught a fever. For two months, I was hardly able to get off my bunk without help.

In October, I received sixty days' furlough to visit our relatives in Mobile. When I reached Demopolis, my hand was hurting badly and I was very unwell. I decided to stop for a few days until I improved. I stayed with an old lady named Brassfield. There, I received all the attention a Confederate soldier could wish for. By the time I was well enough to go on, I had decided to end my journey there rather than go to Mobile [the sentence crosses a damaged edge].

[Scan page 22]

I spent the time very pleasantly and only regretted that I could not stay longer. I shall never forget the kind people of Alabama.

When my furlough expired, I set off for the command. Learning that I could not reach it, I changed direction and went to Columbus, Mississippi, intending to extend my furlough and go on to Mobile. Before I could leave, the Federals destroyed the railroad, preventing me from getting there. I went into the hospital and remained until a few days ago.

My hand has still not healed. The wounds closed on both sides until each opening was about the size of a pea, but they have healed no further. They drain all the time. The doctors say this is caused by diseased bone, or by pieces of bone that ought to have been removed. My wrist will be entirely stiff. I think I will be able to use all my fingers except the middle one.

I returned to the command last week and found the boys in good health. Very few of our brigade are left: only about four hundred out of five regiments.

[Scan page 23; its top strip repeats the opening of page 21]

Only four men in old Company I are left who are fit for duty. One in every seven is being furloughed to go to Texas. Brother Lafayette gets seventy-five days' leave to go home. I am glad he can go and would very much like to accompany him. But I cannot do so honorably, and I would not go home any other way.

I hope you will see Lafayette; he can tell you all the news. I would dearly like to go home. I tried to get a furlough but failed. I shall try to get on the retired list, as I am unfit for field service. If I succeed, I think I will get home by next fall. Even if retired, I would only stay home for nine months. I do not think I would remain there as long as Lafayette is in this army. We have been together all the time, and I think I ought to stay with him. If he became sick or wounded, I would be here to help.

[Scan page 24; its top strip repeats part of page 22]

Lafayette has just returned from the 10th Texas. Squire gets a furlough too. They intended to travel together, but Squire will not get away for a few days, and Lafayette wants to start tomorrow, so they will probably not go together. I will travel with [Lafayette; the original says “you”] as far as Meridian.

I have not heard lately from our cousins, the Jetton boys. Tip is in prison. Billy and Charly were well when last heard from. The 6th Texas boys were all well too.

It is very cold, and I am nearly smoked to death, so I will close for now. Please excuse this hasty, badly written letter. I will try to do better in future. Remember my compliments to all the boys and accept my best wishes for yourself. Write often, and I will do the same. Hoping that we may soon meet again, I remain your true brother,

James [middle initial and surname unclear]

## Pages 27-28
### George tells Zella about the Sylvana meeting

Monday morning

Dear Zella,

Of course, you would like to know about the meeting. Although there have been no further additions, yesterday was a big day. As announced, there was dinner on the grounds—and what a dinner! About the finest ever spread at Sylvana.

Among the visitors were White Stirman and his wife, who sends her love to you, and Nellie Ransdell. They left for Ennis after last night's preaching. White and his family are moving right away to Marlin, where he has a job running a Watkins medicine wagon.

Three carloads came from Oak Cliff. Among them were G. H. Barclay, who kindly remembered you; the wife of J. C. Estes, the preacher; Brother Stevens's mother, wife, and sister; Brother Melton and his family; and Brother Holsenback and his wife.

[Scan page 28]

They thoroughly enjoyed themselves and repeatedly invited me to visit when I am in Dallas. Mrs. Dr. Mitchell and her family, and Brother Dunn's family, came from Waxahachie. These were most of the visitors who stayed for dinner.

After dinner, Mrs. Howard and her family arrived and stayed until after the evening preaching. Everyone who knew her from her time with us showered her with attention. Last came Cliff Stanford, much to his many friends' delight.

The meeting may close tonight or perhaps tomorrow night. Brother Dunn, being something of a revivalist, is discouraged by his work here. He is disappointed.

There is no change in Lucile's condition. I expect to finish this letter at Red Oak this evening and give you the latest news. Larkin is much worse. His disease took a dangerous turn the day before yesterday. I will hear about him this morning. All visitors have been forbidden. The outlook is very serious.

## Pages 29-30
### Crop failure and more news for Zella

[The first surviving line begins mid-letter. August 11 is mentioned, but no year is visible.]

We will have to cut expenses. As of today, August 11, the Ellis County cotton crop is a failure. Worms have completely destroyed all the bolls and buds, and blooms are scarcer than I have ever seen. If anything is produced, it must develop later; there is nothing now. The worms are of every size, which suggests they will be with us for some time yet.

I hope you continue to do well, gain weight and strength, and keep both. I am feeling fine. Looking after myself agrees with me, although I am not particularly fond of it. I am getting two to eight eggs a day.

At Kate's, I found Lucile [a few words are too faint]. She seems quite unwell and is suffering considerably. Kate says, “Her stomach has gone back on her.” Poor child, she looks bad. ## Pages 32-33
### George's troublesome tire and news from home

Wednesday evening

Dear Zella,

### Henry to Lizzie: faith, family, and the harvest, September 6, 1892

[Scan page 87 identifies Henry Douglas, Red Oak, Texas, writing to Sister Lizzie, possibly in California.]

Red Oak, Texas, September 6, 1892

Dear Sister Lizzie,

My long delay in replying was not caused by indifference or lack of affection for a dear absent sister. Some of your letter's contents discouraged me. I thought silence easier and wiser than saying something that might do no good. I wish only to express regret—indeed, sorrow—over the worldly condition described in your letter. Surely California is not unlike Sodom and Gomorrah. A day in the Lord's courts is worth a thousand elsewhere. If I were in such a place, I would hurry away lest I too forget God.

[Scan page 89]

“Be ready, for the Son of Man comes at an hour when you do not expect him” is an instruction we must remember. The wicked may forget, but I know nothing more likely to make us forget than love of money, or the worship of Mammon. We have that here. It keeps Christians away from the Lord's house, weakens faith, dulls conscience, and makes us lazy instead of patiently continuing in good works through which we inherit the promises.

We have just had a good meeting at Sylvana, conducted by Brothers [Art?] and Barnes. You will rejoice with me that Clarence and Kate obeyed the gospel. They will be fourteen on the eighth of this month. I trust that when the Lord gathers his jewels, they will be among them.

[Scan page 90]

Also there were your [nephew?] Otis Mangum, Lee Weatherford, Robbie Sullivan, Jessie Graves, Mrs. Ben Patton, and others you do not know. I wish you could have been with us. I hope you will be here for the next meeting.

We are having fine weather and are gathering good corn. Our wheat and oats were fine, and cotton is a good average. Many are picking; the gin whistles are beginning to sound. If we can get fair prices, we will prosper. Mr. Ward has a good crop for you; I think he is a good renter.

Henry Allen plans to move to Hood County near Acton. Sam Strand has rented Mrs. Bell's place. Mrs. [Fretow?] has rented Dr. Duff's, and her son-in-law, [Arsene?] Henry, our place.

[Scan page 91]

Jodie is in poor health as usual and left yesterday for Erath County. Travis County's crops failed this year, and I do not know whether he will return there. Billy Davis fell from his wagon and [was killed?—the words lie on a dark fold] near Palmer about ten days ago. We have otherwise had a very healthy year so far, exceptionally so.

Give my love to Tom, Laura, and their family, and to John and Sarah. I would like to see them all, but appearances deceive me [the connection in this phrase is uncertain]. As the distance to California is long, our time is growing short. Write when you receive this and say when we may expect you home. Sue will probably teach on Brushy this winter, and I will send Kate to her. Give my regards to Mr. and Mrs. Barton.

Your brother,
Henry

## Pages 92-96
### Henry to his mother: delayed journey home and a church meeting, December 1853

[Scan page 92 labels the letter December 30, 1853. The handwritten heading on page 93 appears to read “Dec. 3d 1853,” meaning December 3. Both readings are preserved for comparison.]

Palestine, December 3 [label: December 30], 1853

Dear Mother,

Time passes rapidly, and tonight I find myself writing instead of traveling home as I had wished. I often think definite plans for the future are hardly worth making, since mine fail so often. The date of my return is so uncertain that this time I will make no promises. The main reason for the delay is that my expenses have been greater than expected, so I must work longer.

I hoped to see Warren here last week but was disappointed. I did have the pleasure of meeting David Geaslin at Bonner's Ferry on the Trinity last Monday evening and again Tuesday morning. From him I learned some news of home, including that Jesse and Warren had gone out to the Brazos.

[Scan page 94]

I am very much behind on your plans and prospects at home. None of you writes anything definite about them. Warren has written a little about his trip to the Brazos, but referred only casually to a second trip in his last letter. Had I come home now, I suppose I would have had the pleasure of missing both him and Jesse. When I do come, I want them both to be there. Please write immediately and tell me when they will return.

That is all my business for now; I must think of some news. Our meeting closed last Monday night. I think it was very useful and interesting. Because of cold, bad weather, neighboring churches were less fully represented than expected. I think the cooperation will be effective. About $450 was pledged for evangelism, and with a little effort I believe that amount could be doubled.

[Scan page 95]

A committee of five managers was appointed to employ an evangelist and conduct the cooperation's other business. We hope much good will result in the coming year. Several preachers were confidently expected, but none came, so Brother Kendrick did all the preaching.

Five were baptized, and another person was added to the church. Two of them were little boys: Addilann [name uncertain], Brother Clark's son, about twelve; and Albert, Brother K.'s eldest son, about ten. They seemed fully to understand what they were doing, and I have no doubt they did. It was a beautiful and moving scene: young, comparatively innocent children entering the Kingdom and dedicating their lives to the Redeemer, while old, gray-haired people remained unmoved by the gospel and could not be persuaded to devote what remained of their seventy years to the only cause worth living for and the only one promising anything for the future.

[Scan page 96]

There is considerable sickness in the country, and I occasionally hear of a death. My own health is excellent. My paper is nearly full, and I must close.

I now think I will probably be home around Christmas or early in January. My love to all.

Yours affectionately,
Henry Douglas
