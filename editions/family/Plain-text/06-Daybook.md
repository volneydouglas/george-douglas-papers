## 08-001 — Repository cover

ACU’s cover identifies this file as Folder 5, the daybook of N. L. Douglas, with the catalog date 1830. The collection is held by Abilene Christian University and available through Digital Commons at ACU.

<a id="s08p2"></a>

## 08-002 — Identification of the daybook

A later typed note identifies this as Nathaniel Lynn Douglas’s daybook, beginning January 4, 1830, and associates him with Murfreesboro, Tennessee, and Liberty Settlement and Red Oak in Ellis County, Texas.

<a id="s08p3"></a>

## 08-003 — Names and family notes on the cover

A note laid over the cover says that Bruce [Headrick?] was a great-grandson of the late N. L. Douglas. Other writing on the cover names N. L. Douglas, “Grandfather,” and C. C. Douglas. A lower note refers to settlement in the 1840s on Brushy Creek and land then owned by Bruce [Headrick?]. Several words, including the end of the lowest line, remain uncertain.

## 08-096 — A land warrant sold and news of cholera, June 5, 1854

Murfreesboro, Rutherford County, Tennessee, June 5, 1854.
Mr. N. L. Douglass, my old and trusted friend,
Your letter dated April 25 reached me on May 27. It enclosed a properly transferred warrant for eighty acres of land and asked me to sell it for the best price I could obtain and pay the proceeds to Mr. G. A. Sublett. I carried out your request as promptly as circumstances allowed. His receipt, enclosed here, will show you this.

I sent the warrant to Nashville. Ninety dollars is the highest price obtainable in that market, while speculators obtain ninety-three in New York. I have kept a copy of the receipt in case the original is lost in the mail or another accident occurs.

Our old friend Sublett remains in poor health. I think his constitution is exhausted and that he will never regain anything like his former health.

Cholera appeared in Nashville around the twenty-sixth of last month and also in several places in this county, especially its western end adjoining Davidson County. Fifteen or twenty people died in Nashville in the first two or three days of the outbreak, and about the same number died in this county. It has eased somewhat as the weather has become more favorable to health, but the community remains afraid.

Reading note: The warrant concerns eighty acres; $90 is its reported sale-market value. The letter’s disease explanation and mortality estimates are preserved as historical testimony, not modern medical findings.

## 08-097 — News of the Ott family, an inheritance dispute and a criminal trial

Anderson Ott has sold his small farm near town and bought about 150 acres ten miles southeast of town on the Manchester turnpike. He and his family are well. Nat Ott runs a shop, but his health is very delicate. A year ago last spring he had a dangerous hemorrhage from his lungs, and it returned slightly this spring. ## 08-124 — A difficult start after army service, and Jetton’s view of Reconstruction

[[He has had]] a poor start in life. He was put into the army when he should have been at school. I opposed his going to Texas. ## 08-138 — Historical notes on managing community property after a spouse’s death

Section 33. When a wife dies and her husband survives, formal estate administration is unnecessary except for any separate property she owned. The husband keeps the authority over community property that he held during the marriage. He must nevertheless submit an inventory and valuation of it and file a bond, with one or more guarantors, approved by and payable to the county’s district clerk. The bond must equal the whole value of the community property. It guarantees that he will manage the property faithfully and, after paying debts properly chargeable against it, turn over half the remaining surplus to the people entitled to it.

Section 34. The husband may be required to account for his management at any time after one year from the bond’s date.

Section 35. The bond may be sued upon and recovered on in the same way as an administrator’s bond, and is otherwise treated the same.

Section 36. A surviving wife may keep sole management and control of the couple’s community property under the same conditions, rights and rules described in the preceding three sections until she remarries. On remarriage she loses [[continues]].

Reading note: This is a readable version of a historical legal excerpt, not current legal guidance or a determination about a particular estate.

## 08-164 — Nance asks whether Douglas helped free Sam, June 1867

Quincy, Tennessee, June 5, 1867.
N. L. Douglass—Sir,
A short time ago, I received your very short letter with a receipt. I have one question: Did you help in any way to free Sam, the enslaved man? I want to know for certain. You know the answer, and please tell me. If your answer is yes, I will send your receipt back. If not, I will make every effort to send your money. I am quite able to pay the amount claimed.

You and I are getting old, and our earthly interests will soon end, so I do not want to do wrong. What I mean is this: if you helped take what I regarded as my property from me, you can get your money through legal proceedings. If not, I will gladly send it to you.

Reading note: Nance speaks of Sam as property and makes repayment conditional on Douglas’s role in his freedom. This letter names Sam but does not give his surname, tell how he became free, or establish whether Douglas assisted him. No answer is present on this page.

## 08-167 — N. L. Douglas’s oath of allegiance and office

Oath of Office.
I, N. L. Douglas, solemnly swear that since becoming a United States citizen, I have never voluntarily taken up arms against the United States. I have voluntarily given no help, approval, advice or encouragement to people fighting it. I have neither sought nor accepted an office, nor tried to perform the duties of any office, under an authority claiming power in opposition to the United States. I have not voluntarily supported any claimed government, authority, power or constitution within the United States that was hostile to it.

I further swear that, to the best of my knowledge and ability, I will support and defend the United States Constitution against all foreign and domestic enemies. I will remain faithful and loyal to it. I take this obligation freely, without hidden reservations or intent to evade it, and will faithfully perform the duties of the office I am about to enter. So help me God.

Reading note: The reverse dates this paper to January 1868. This is the wording of an oath in Douglas’s name; the scan does not show a witness, certification or separate signature. It is evidence of the declaration he recorded, not independent proof of every claim in it.
