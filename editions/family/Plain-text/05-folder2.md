## Pages 3-4
### Henry Douglas writes to his father about his medical practice

[Scan page 3]

Palestine, Texas, January 12, 1855

Dear Father,

I have been looking for a letter from home for a long time, having heard from none of you since I left [them?] in October. But as I have not heard, I [propose?] to write again, for [illegible phrase]. I think it rather uncertain when you would hear from me. In case any of my recent letters have failed to reach you, I should say that I have written several times since November. This must excuse me if that is what happened.

I very much wanted a letter from you before making arrangements for this year, and waited until New Year's Day for that purpose. Then, left entirely to my own judgment, I decided to remain here another year and risk the consequences. Dr. Kendrick and I have formed a partnership for the year. I receive one-third of the accounts and pay one-third of the expenses, as well as my own board, and provide my own horse.

The advantages of this arrangement are his experience, a share in a firmly established practice, and shared responsibility in difficult cases, which is no small consideration. The disadvantages are:

[Scan page 4]

The possibility of making more money elsewhere; the greater incentive to study if I had to rely on my own resources; and perhaps the loss of a good place to practice by arriving a year later. Have I acted wisely or not?

We have great opposition [remaining words unclear], both from numbers and prejudice. However, this prejudice is giving way in people's minds. As for the doctors, it makes very little difference what they think, say, or do. Dr. K.'s practice has been steadily increasing for some time and will continue to do so. Old Mr. Ranson, whom I take to be a man of strong prejudices, has declared that he intends to try Dr. K. the next time he needs a physician. Others say the same. I mention him because you know him.

I wrote to Warren about a cancer case we are treating. It was considered doubtful, and probably still is, but the patient is now doing well and we have more hope. It was a great risk, but will be a great triumph if a cure is achieved.

My health is good. I think I can venture to promise you a visit in the spring, perhaps April. I could come sooner, but the winter winds and sleet are too bad. If you have not written, please do so immediately. My love to all.

Yours with all affection,
Henry Douglas

[Addressed at the foot to N. L. Douglas.]

## Pages 6-7
### Henry Douglas writes to his mother about coming home

[Scan page 6]

Palestine, Texas, April 8, 1853

Dear Mother,

The letter from you and Susannah came by the last mail. Since you may be somewhat disappointed by the delay in my promised visit, I am writing a few lines.

I received a letter from Uncle Sam a short time ago suggesting May 1 as the best time for me to come up, because of his court commitments in April and Jesse's and Warren's absence. He thought they probably would not be home until about then. As it made no significant difference to me, I wrote him immediately that I would come during the last week of April. In case that letter should go astray, I am writing this one. Your letter was nearly three weeks on the way. If this one travels no faster, and nothing prevents me, I will be with you soon after you receive it.

I am pleased that Susannah has agreed to come down with me. I hope there will be no disappointment of any kind, and that the step will prove entirely satisfactory to everyone concerned. I think you are inclined to praise my humble self far too highly in this matter. I have only done what seemed to me a reasonable duty. I feel that I have been blessed beyond my expectations in whatever

[Scan page 7]

I have undertaken since I left your home to seek my fortune in the wider world. As long as memory lasts, I will never forget the day I parted from Father on the wide, lonely prairie near the Willow pond: his parting advice, and the loneliness of the hours that followed as I walked to Porter's Bluff. Never before had I felt so isolated. Never had I known what it was to be homeless, without a roof, and effectively friendless. I was going among strangers and did not know where. I took everything on trust, and I have not been disappointed. I have been blessed with health and plenty, not as abundantly as some, but as much as I had any right to hope for. I repeat that I deserve no praise. If I can help others, I am only doing my duty.

You may therefore expect me toward the end of April, though of course I cannot say precisely when. I hope to stay with you for a week or ten days, perhaps longer. The weather here has been very dry for a long time, and the crops are suffering badly for rain. The corn has been badly frostbitten, and much of it will have to be planted again. I fear that your wheat has been ruined.

I remain, as ever, your affectionate son,
Henry Douglas

[Addressed at the foot to Mrs. Mary Douglas.]

P.S. Give my love to all. Remember me to Mr. Orr and Aunt Cynthia, and so forth.
H. D.

## Pages 9-11
### Henry Douglas describes the difficulties of a sheep drive

[Scan page 9]

Mississippi County, Missouri, June 23, 1860

Dear Father,

After a long interval, I now have an opportunity to write to you. I have nothing encouraging to report. I have had to pay from one to two dollars for sheep, have had a great deal of trouble finding them, and have incurred expenses in collecting my flock. Mr. Willis [Holm?] sent me $500 to get him some sheep. With the $1,500 I have bought 750 head, and I do not know how I will have enough money to get them to Texas, as I have only about $200 left.

I bought common sheep in Callaway and Graves Counties, Kentucky: lambs at $1 and ewes at $1.25 a head. But I could not get enough, so I went back to Christian County and got about 250 head at $1.75 and $2 a head. These are finer sheep, and I would now prefer to have invested all my funds in sheep like them.

I crossed the Mississippi at the Iron Banks last Sunday and had to pay eight cents a head for the ferry. After driving for two days, I had to stop because the sheep were getting a disease from poison or something else. Several have died. Altogether I have lost nearly fifty head since I started, mostly because they slipped away into the thickets during the first few days'

[Scan page 10]

drive. I learn that every flock that has passed through here has suffered severely in the same way. Last year, about this time, a Mr. Long passed through with 1,000 and lost 150 to 200. A Mr. [South?], a Methodist preacher, with 300 or 400, lost only a few. He passed through about the end of July. A few days ago a flock of 700 or 800 passed from Rutherford County, Tennessee, and lost a great many. People here say that I have escaped more lightly than any of them. They also say that all the flocks did well after leaving here, which I greatly hope is true.

I now have my sheep in a large crabgrass pasture, free of charge, and will remain until I think it prudent to start. I hope that will be in three or four days. Nearly all are doing well, but perhaps fifty show signs of disease. The first symptoms are drooping and swelling of the ears, which thicken as the tissue fills with yellow fluid. The sheep become dull and sluggish. In some cases the head swells; the skin hangs in a pouch under the jaw. I have stuck my knife into several, and the yellow fluid flowed freely. I know I have lost five since crossing the river, and one more will probably die.

I forgot to say that, after the head swells, the sheep appear to suffer intolerable itching, judging from their behavior. Their hair comes off when the swelling goes down. I have two tollgates

[Scan page 11]

to pass before I get clear of the swamps. After that, I suppose there will be no more until I reach the Fort Gibson ferry on the Arkansas. I have four hired men at $15 a month each. Taking everything together, I cannot make the drive profitable, if I do not actually lose money on it.

I have not heard from any of you since I left Texas, nor from Ellen since I left Lincoln County. Taking everything into consideration, I cannot help feeling rather downhearted. But win or lose, I am bound to go through with it. My health is still good, and if I do not get sick, I think I can stand it.

Now that I have written this, I do not know that it would be best to send it. But you had better know the truth than remain in suspense. I suppose I will reach Springfield, Missouri, in thirty or forty days. You must write to me there, and also at Fort Gibson. Write to Warren to do the same, as I am very anxious to hear from all of you.

I have been much disappointed and troubled by men who agreed to make the trip with me but failed to arrive at the appointed time; some did not come at all. This forced me to hire temporary hands at higher prices and pay them off when they quit, which has been a heavy drain on my pocket. I will write again. For now, this is enough. Love to all.

Your affectionate son,
H. Douglas

## Pages 14-15
### James Bass asks about land and taxes

[Scan page 14]

Murfreesboro, Tennessee, April 18, 1854

My dear sir,

It has been about two years since I heard from you about our business. I believe it has been nearly three years. I would be glad to receive a letter from you giving me a full account of everything, and I hope you will write immediately upon receiving this.

I have recently bought from Governor A. [Gullett?] the remainder of the Littleton White tract of land. The purchase includes about 3,250 acres. You know where it is, and I want you to attend to it. See whether the taxes have been paid, and give me any information you can obtain about it.

I would be obliged if you would write fully about the Levi Dickson tract on the Trinity River. I believe it is called the Levi Dickson tract. Governor A. [Gullett?] owns it, and I am thinking of buying it. I believe the land is at the mouth of Five Mile Creek [the word “creek” replaces a crossed-out word]. Write to tell me what the land is worth and give me any information you have or can obtain about it.

[Scan page 15]

I expect to be in Texas in the fall and will see you.

I am your friend and obedient servant,
James Bass

[Address panel]
Nathaniel Douglas
Red Oak Post Office
Texas

### A poem on loneliness

[A typed copy on the scanned sheet, attributed to G. Douglas, 1867. Rendered in plain English.]

There is a loneliness of heart
That no words can ever express,
Too deep for sorrow's tears to start
And ease the aching breast:
A sunless, motionless, waveless waste,
All dark and drearily forlorn;
And every lovely flower placed there
Conceals a poisonous thorn.

1867, G. Douglas

## Pages 47-48
### Sadie describes a picnic and the children's remarks

[Scan page 47]

Bardsdale, August 8

Dearest Aunt,

I have just finished getting dinner out. Mr. Headrick has gone to work. Elmo is seated on the table and Bruce beside it, [with a cold?], talking away. I am as well as usual. Mr. H. helped me wash yesterday.

Grandma Daugherty's growth has increased, making it so difficult for her to breathe that she has consented to an operation. I am very afraid she will not survive it. Dr. Kingsley is the head man, and Drs. Bard and Mott are to do the work.

I have seen Dr. H. passing once since his return. I warmly told him I was glad to see him back and expressed my lasting gratitude for his kindness. I could hardly keep from saying that if he thought half as much of me as I did of him, he would have called sooner. But we cannot express ourselves freely to strangers.

There was a picnic at Mr. Timm's last Saturday, quite a success. I prepared a good dinner in case I had a chance to go, but the starting hour came with no opportunity. How fully I realized that if I were back there, or even if you were here, it would be different!

If I had acted on my own feelings, I would have made no effort to go. But the children were so anxious that they said if I didn't go down and ask Mr. Wigmore for his cart, they would. I knew they couldn't bring it alone, so I went with them. Fortunately for my [haggard?] face, it had already been spoken for, and I found that out before asking.

I found Mrs. Pickering hardly out of bed, still preparing to go camping in the hope of being able to stay a little longer with her children. I swept her house, baked two jelly cakes, and came home about noon.

Mr. H. took his dinner, so the children and I ate our picnic dinner alone. After finishing, I sat down to read a little. Then Mrs. Henry Chadsey came down in Mrs. Vernon's surrey to get us. Lillie Chadsey had asked several people why I wasn't there, and—

[Scan page 48]

—everyone's answer was, of course, “I guess she didn't have any way.” She told them there was no reason I shouldn't have a way when there were so many vehicles there. She hurriedly arranged to send for us. I was thankful, especially for the children.

They had a large table heavily loaded with the choicest food. Mrs. Timm's took almost all her dishes, and everyone else took plenty. It was a set table, just like one at home.
